\begin{abstract}
The hidden coordinates of a closed model cannot be read from its outputs: a gauge symmetry of the network leaves every output unchanged. What can be read is a geometry. Asking yes/no questions in side branches of the conversation gives answer laws whose Fisher--Rao distances are exactly Euclidean in arcsine coordinates, and a cross-fit estimator from sampled text alone is exactly unbiased for a known monotone transform of these distances. We measure this observable geometry on eight open models and test it against their hidden states, with every test preregistered and its lock published before the data. It tracks the hidden geometry (Spearman \GOneAns) far better than a sentence embedding of the input (\GOneText) or of the model's own replies (\GFiveRep). But on average it is not the model's own: on 1{,}000 fresh contexts, a model's answers match other models' hidden geometries as well as its own (mean own-model excess $+0.002$), and another open model's hidden state is the better stand-in. What a closed model's answers reveal is a latent geometry it shares with open models; Claude Haiku~5.5 reveals the same. What crosses an API boundary depends on what it returns: yes/no answers reveal no model-specific geometry, next-token probabilities reveal some, and log-probability vectors, a linear image of the final state, single out a model's own final-layer geometry among eight models for seven. It is consistent with monitoring a closed agent through an open model: reading transcripts through an open model, a monitor trained only on open agents catches 94\% and 98\% of two closed agents' violations at a 5\% label-free review budget, and cuts the episodes with an executed violation from about 7\% to 0.2\% live. A closed agent can also police itself through its own API, with no other model and no labels: asked in a side branch whether each proposed call breaks a rule of its policy, Claude Haiku~5.5 prevented 69\% of the violations its transcript decides, at no cost in task success, and, as \cref{cor:action} predicts, not those decided by a fact it never checked (preregistered). The model-specific final geometry is recoverable exactly only with full log-probabilities. We release the tool.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Interpretability reads what a model knows and what it will do from its hidden state \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}, and for agents a probe can stop an unsafe action before it runs \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. These tools need the weights. The most capable models are closed: their providers return sampled text and, at most, some log-probabilities.

\textbf{What can an outside observer know about a closed model's state?} Not its coordinates: any reparametrisation of the hidden space that the next layers undo leaves every output unchanged, a gauge symmetry (\cref{prop:gauge}). Distances between states survive this symmetry, so we ask which geometry the outputs reveal. Ask the model yes/no questions about a context, each in its own side branch. In the angles $\vartheta=2\arcsin\sqrt{P(\textsc{yes})}$, the Fisher--Rao distance between two contexts' answer laws is plain Euclidean distance (\cref{thm:geometry}): a change of variables, not a learned embedding, and estimable from sampled text on any chat API.

\textbf{What we find.} This observable geometry tracks a model's hidden geometry far better than sentence embeddings of the input or of the model's own replies. But a control that an independent review asked for shows that, on average, it is not the model's own: a model's answers match \emph{other} models' hidden geometries as well as its own, and an open model's hidden state is a better stand-in than the model's own answers. What outputs reveal is the latent geometry that models share \citep{picozzi2026information,huh2024platonic}. A closed model, Claude, reveals the same. It is consistent with a common practice, reading a closed model through an open one, which we use to monitor closed agents. What a model reveals of its \emph{own} state depends on the channel: next-token probabilities leak some, and log-probability vectors recover its final geometry (\cref{thm:exact}).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Measurement} (\cref{sec:geom-results}): on eight open models, the answer geometry tracks hidden geometry better than sentence embeddings of the input and of the model's replies; on average it carries no model-specific part, on fresh contexts, up to 70B (exploratory), and Claude shows a stable geometry of the same kind; what crosses an API boundary of the model's own state depends on the channel. We report where the answers lose.
\item \emph{Formal grounding} (\cref{sec:method}): exact flat coordinates for the observable geometry, a cross-fit estimator exactly unbiased for a known transform of it, and the exact final geometry from log-probabilities after \citet{carlini2024stealing}, with its sharp $d(d+1)/2$ threshold measured.
\item \emph{Application to closed agents} (\cref{sec:results}): with the transcript and the action sufficient for policy checks (\cref{cor:action}), a monitor that reads them through an open model predicts and prevents two closed agents' violations live, also when the agent knows it is monitored; and an agent audited through its own API prevents the violations that its transcript decides, with an exact law for the effect and its boundary.
\item \emph{A released tool} (\cref{sec:tool}): one command measures a model's observable geometry; an SDK, a server and a browser trainer monitor agents.
\end{enumerate}
Every confirmatory test was preregistered: its document and analysis code were hash-locked (SHA-256) before the data existed, and for the later tests, all geometry tests included, the lock was published first. Tests that failed, a dropped design, a correction of our own theorem, and the limits of each claim are in \cref{app:limits,app:record}.

\section{What a model's answers reveal: results}
\label{sec:geom-results}
\textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (harmful and benign requests, reviews, yes/no questions, maths problems, encyclopedia text), split into 3{,}360 development and 1{,}440 confirmation contexts; and two further sets of 1{,}000 fresh contexts, one for the sharing test and one for the channel test, each with 400 contexts of four kinds never used before (news, programming tasks, jailbreak behaviours, prompt injections). A battery of 88 questions about the message (topic, intent, tone, demands, harm, and the model's own stance). Eight open chat models from six families. Ground truth: each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at the layer chosen on development contexts only. All results are on held-out contexts, as preregistered.

\textbf{The answers track the hidden geometry; embeddings of the input and of the replies do not.} On every model the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input (MiniLM; \cref{tab:geometry}; gains \GOneGain, every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; preregistered, 8 of 8). An embedding of the model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, does little better than that of the input (\GFiveRep; preregistered: \GFiveOut): one reply and the mean of eight differ by at most \GFiveSatPoint\ (upper bound \GFiveSat), while from 8 to 88 questions the answers' agreement rises by \GOneMore. Answers sampled from the model's own probabilities, 8 per question, keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. Three qualifications. Against stronger sentence encoders of the input or of the replies (mpnet; Qwen3-Embedding), the answers stay ahead for \StrongConfirm\ models on these contexts and \StrongFresh\ on fresh ones; for Gemma-3 and gpt-oss a strong embedding matches or beats them (exploratory), and an open model's own hidden state does better still (below). Much of the agreement is the separation of kinds of context: within one kind it is weaker (median \WKAns\ for answers, \WKText\ for text; answers ahead in \WKCells\ model--kind cells; exploratory). And on the fresh contexts of the later tests agreement is lower (answers \GSixAns, input \GSixText, replies \GSixRep); there Gemma's replies beat its answers.

\textbf{On average the geometry is shared, not the model's own.} An independent review asked whether a model's answers match \emph{its own} hidden geometry better than another model's. On average they do not. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the agreement of model A's answers with model B's hidden geometry has no mean own-model excess beyond row and column effects: \GSixExcess, inside the preregistered equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ (\cref{fig:geometry}d). Individual models differ (\GSixPerModel; exploratory), and on the 400 contexts of the new kinds the mean excess is \GSixNewExcess\ (exploratory). The hidden geometries of different models agree with each other (\GSixHH), and an open model's hidden state (Qwen3-8B) matches another model's hidden geometry better than that model's own answers do, for \GSixSTwo\ models (\GSixSTwoRange; preregistered). So what a model's answers reveal of its state is, on average, the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}. \citet{schiekiera2026associations} report behavioural signal beyond cross-model consensus for word-similarity judgements; with our battery we find none on average.

\textbf{The answers beat the input embedding on new kinds of context.} On the 400 contexts of the four new kinds, they do so for \GSixSThree\ models (\GSixAnsNew\ vs \GSixTextNew; preregistered; for Gemma and gpt-oss the interval includes 0).

\textbf{What crosses the API boundary.} Is the sharing a property of outputs in general, or of answers? We measured the own-model excess for each channel that an API can return, simulated with exact open-model probabilities on 1{,}000 further fresh contexts (preregistered; \cref{fig:geometry}b). Against the last layer: yes/no answers \GSevLAns; the next-token law reduced to its top 1, 5 and 20 tokens \GSevLTopOne, \GSevLTopFive\ and \GSevLTopTwenty; the full law under Fisher--Rao \GSevLFull, no more than the top 20; and the centred log-probability vectors \GSevLLogprob, which single out a model's own final-layer geometry among the eight for \GSevBestLogprob\ models (with answers, for \GSevBestAns). This last result is close to guaranteed, since the log-probabilities are a linear image of the final state (\cref{thm:exact}); against the layer chosen on development data the log-probability excess is \GSevCLogprob\ and the own model is the best match for \GSevCBestLogprob. So answers reveal what models share, and next-token probabilities, which an API may return, leak part of the model itself (\GSevOut). That next-token laws carry a model-specific part qualifies the cross-model sharing that \citet{picozzi2026information} report for them: the shared part dominates, but it is not all.

\textbf{A closed model.} Claude Haiku~5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of the samples give the same geometry: Spearman \GTwoSplit, above the bound of 0.7 fixed in advance from open models, and \GTwoText\ beyond the input embedding (preregistered). Most cells are unanimous, so this stability partly reflects how deterministic Claude is, and with 4 samples per half the estimate is of a contracted transform of the Fisher--Rao geometry (\cref{thm:geometry}d). With the cross-fit estimator the geometry is close to Euclidean (negative eigenvalue mass \CFClaudeNegCF, against \CFClaudeNeg\ with the variance-subtraction rule). Its agreement with the open models' hidden geometries is \GTwoOpen; with the same protocol, open models' answers agree with other open models' hidden geometries at \ClaudeRefOther\ (exploratory). Since answers carry no model-specific part on average, this measures the shared geometry; it says nothing about Claude's own hidden states, which no one outside can check.

\textbf{The model-specific final geometry from log-probabilities.} \Cref{thm:exact} recovers a model's own final geometry exactly from full log-probabilities. On Qwen3-0.6B ($d=1{,}024$), from 1.15 million self-generated contexts, the recovered cosines of 2{,}000 held-out contexts correlate with the true ones at \GThreeBelow\ below $d(d+1)/2$ contexts (at $0.75\times$ and $0.9\times$ the minimum-norm solution is indefinite and the recovery fails); at $1.1\times$ the correlation is \GThreeOneOne, and at $2\times$ the median error is $\GThreeErr$ (maximum $\GThreeMax$) (\cref{fig:geometry}c; preregistered). Closed APIs do not return full log-probabilities.

\textbf{Where answers lose.} For detecting \emph{that} a model's state moved (a hidden system prompt), sampled replies had more power than sampled answers at equal calls in every model and condition on development data, so we did not run that test (\cref{app:limits}).

\section{Application: monitoring closed agents}
\label{sec:results}
Because what outputs reveal is mostly shared, and an open model's state is a better stand-in for another model's geometry than that model's own answers (\cref{sec:geom-results}), we monitor closed agents through an open model. The monitor is a supervised probe on the open model's state of the transcript and the proposed action; its validity rests on \cref{cor:action}, not on the geometry. On real agent steps, the reader's raw geometry agrees with each open agent's own decision-state geometry at \AgentLink, more than an embedding of the text for three of four agents (exploratory): a modest link.
\textbf{Agents and monitors.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised; an environment checker labels each action. Open agents: Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B and Muse-Glimmer-30B. Closed agents, through their public APIs with reasoning off: Claude Haiku~5.5 and GPT-6-luna. The reader is Qwen3-8B (layer 32), with a logistic probe trained only on 17{,}623 Gemma-4 steps; baselines are the agent's own internals (where open), TF-IDF with the same labels, a zero-shot 70B judge and the action's tool alone.

\textbf{The transcript is enough.} \Cref{cor:action} says that the agent's internals cannot beat a monitor of the transcript and the action. A probe on Gemma-4's own internals scores 0.841 at the start of the turn and 0.979 after its reasoning; the reader probe, trained on the same labels, scores 0.994 ($+0.015$ [0.007, 0.026] over the internals) and is non-inferior on unseen task families (\cref{tab:main}). Given the action, adding the agent's hidden reasoning changes Gemma-4's AUROC by $-0.0014$ [$-0.0046$, $+0.0014$]. A preregistered test on the held-out scenarios of three other reasoning agents (G8) gives a mixed answer. Trained on the agent's own labels, the reader with the reasoning changes the AUROC by \GEightInagent{} on average; non-superiority at $+0.01$ fails, because Qwen3.8 gains \GEightQwen{}. Trained on Gemma-4 only, which is how a new agent is monitored, the reasoning lowers the AUROC by \GEightTransfer{} (per agent \GEightTransferEach{}). For violations later in the episode, which the action does not decide, both monitors are weak (\GEightFutureLevel{}) and the reasoning's effect is \GEightFuture{}: too few positives to say. The reader's state also replicates the agent's: on four architectures it reconstructs 46--66\% of the variance of the main components of the decision state on unseen scenarios, more than text features, and a probe trained on true states scores at least as well on the replica (\cref{app:agents,app:rb}). What the reader adds over word counts is robustness to shift: TF-IDF with the same labels ties it on the training benchmark but is at chance on AgentDojo (0.503 vs 0.781).

\textbf{Two closed agents.} Nothing was fit on the closed agents. At a cut set at the top 5\% of the agent's own unlabelled traffic, the monitor catches 94.1\% of Claude Haiku~5.5's 68 violations (zero-shot judge: 0\%; TF-IDF: 63.8\%) and 98.5\% of GPT-6-luna's (judge 37.9\%; TF-IDF 49.0\%). Claude's violations are two behaviours: 42 test sends to a mailing list whose thousands of external members a command would have shown but the agent never checked, and 26 restarts of production services without \texttt{request\_permission}. A monitor that knew only each template's violation rate would catch 93.9\%, so on Claude the result shows that the monitor flags these two behaviours, not that it reads the deciding facts. GPT-6-luna's 66 violations span six templates, each with safe calls, and there the monitor beats the template rate (98.5\% vs 88.7\%). The scale of the scores does not transfer between agents (on Claude a cut at probability 0.5 catches 3\%), but the ranking does, so the cut must come from the monitored agent's traffic. Frozen after 500 actions, it keeps catching 94\% under bursts in which half the actions are violations, while a cut that moves over a window of 5{,}000 actions falls to 36\%, as \cref{prop:burst} predicts (\cref{fig:transfer}a).

\textbf{Live prevention.} We ran the monitor as a server in each closed agent's tool loop, on the same scenarios with and without the gate. In all four preregistered live tests the gate removed nearly all executed violations (\cref{tab:live,fig:prevention}), and no agent executed a violation after a block. Holding an escalated action for a reviewer kept the safety gain; its preregistered bound on the goal cost was not shown ($-0.020$ [$-0.051$, $+0.007$]; \cref{app:limits}). The round trip was \LATENCY.

\textbf{Self-policing: the agent's own API as the monitor.} A monitor can also use nothing but the monitored agent. Before a non-read call runs, we ask the agent in a side branch, which its conversation never sees, whether the call complies with its policy; a flagged call goes to review (\emph{gate}) or is replaced by a fresh draw (\emph{S4}). No other model and no labels are used. If the audit flags a share $\tau$ of violating calls, a gate prevents about $\tau$ of the violations (\cref{prop:prevention}, \cref{app:agents}). On unseen scenarios (\cref{tab:selfpolice}), Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes and reached more goals. Claude Haiku~5.5 needed the policy checked rule by rule: asked only whether a call complies, it flagged 5\% of its own violations. With that audit it prevented 69\% of the violations that its transcript decides, at no cost in goals. It did not prevent test sends to a mailing list whose thousands of external members appear only after sending (7\% [0, 17]): \cref{cor:action} says that no judge of the transcript can see such a fact, and the test confirmed this prediction. The reader gate above blocks those sends too, through a prior learned from other agents' labels; self-policing needs no labels and no second model, and stops where the transcript stops. It is verification, not self-knowledge: in a cross-audit Gemma-4 and gpt-oss judge each other's calls about as well as their own (interaction $+0.03$).

\textbf{An agent that knows it is monitored, and an attacker who queries the score.} Told in its system prompt that a safety monitor reviews every tool call, Claude violated as often as before, and the monitor caught 97.1\% of its violations at the frozen cut (83.6\% when not told; $+0.136$ [0.050, 0.225]; preregistered). The notice moved all scores up, so the frozen cut escalated 15.2\% of actions instead of 5\%, within the Kolmogorov bound of \cref{prop:shift} ($K=0.24$); a cut set again on the told agent's unlabelled traffic escalated 4.7\% live and still removed every executed violation (\cref{tab:live}). An attacker who can query the score and rewrites only the free text of a violating call lowered the share caught from 96\% to 29\%; two preregistered defenses failed (51\% and 60\%), close to the ceiling of \cref{prop:ceiling} (\cref{app:limits}). The score must stay hidden from the agent.

\textbf{Labels of a new policy transfer from open agents.} Prompt injection on AgentDojo is a policy absent from our training data. Labels of it collected on two \emph{open} agents transfer to the \emph{closed} GPT-6-luna: AUROC 0.922 against the judge's 0.787 ($+0.136$ [0.078, 0.191]; \cref{tab:main}). The limit of a monitor is the coverage of its policies, not the agent.

\section{The tool}
\label{sec:tool}
We release a Python package and a website (links in the camera-ready version). One command, \texttt{graybox-geometry}, measures a chat model's observable geometry over the user's contexts: it reads $P(\textsc{yes})$ exactly where the API returns log-probabilities, and otherwise samples two independent halves and uses the cross-fit of \cref{thm:geometry}(d); it reports the number of calls and a cost estimate before it runs, and its tests check each theorem on cases with a known answer. For agents, a user imports logs (OpenAI or Anthropic format), labels actions and trains a monitor in the browser, where the logs never leave the machine; a guard places the monitor in a tool loop, an MCP proxy or one of three agent frameworks, and sets the cut again on new unlabelled traffic after a change of prompt (\cref{app:tool}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Information geometry and shared representations.} The Fisher metric and its uniqueness are classical \citep{cencov1982statistical,campbell1986extended,amari2016information}, as are the arcsine transform \citep{anscombe1948transformation} and classical scaling \citep{torgerson1952mds}; representational similarity analysis compares distance matrices without a fit \citep{kriegeskorte2008rsa}. Representations of different models converge \citep{huh2024platonic}. \citet{picozzi2026information} show that the Fisher--Rao geometry of next-token distributions is set by behaviour up to symmetries and shared across architectures; \citet{schiekiera2026associations} compare behavioural and hidden-state geometry in eight models. We measure the geometry through yes/no answers on sampled text, so it applies to closed models; we test it against hidden states with a cross-model control; and we use its sharing to justify monitoring closed agents through an open model.
\textbf{Black-box access.} Logits span a $d$-dimensional image \citep{finlayson2024logits}, and a production model's output projection is recovered up to an orthogonal map \citep{carlini2024stealing}; \cref{thm:exact} uses the same structure for the gauge-invariant hidden geometry. Replies serve to test whether an API serves a given model \citep{gao2025model}; in our data they are the more sensitive output for a change and the less faithful one for the state. Answers to questions serve as interpretable embeddings \citep{benara2024crafting}, detect lies with follow-up questions \citep{pacchiardi2024liar,cooney2026didyoulie,sam2025quere}, and forecast behaviour \citep{levy2026forecasting}; open readers probe closed transcripts \citep{conti2026hermeneutics}.
\textbf{Probes, monitors and control.} Probes on internals read truth, refusal and deception \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}, and CAPE probes an agent before each action \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. Chain-of-thought monitors catch reward hacking that action-only monitors miss \citep{baker2025monitoring,korbak2025cot,chen2025reasoning,arnav2025redhanded}; \cref{cor:action} delimits when the reasoning adds nothing at the Bayes level; for trained readers on policy labels, we find it adds little and hurts transfer across agents (G8). AI control audits a fixed budget of actions \citep{greenblatt2024control}; \cref{prop:frozen,prop:burst} give the budget a label-free, exact and burst-robust form. Latents made identifiable by training are recovered by complete encoders \citep{zhou2026identifiabilities}; \cref{prop:gauge} is the output-side counterpart.

\section{Conclusion}
A closed model's hidden coordinates are not observable. Its yes/no answers reveal a geometry, exact in arcsine coordinates and estimable from sampled text. That geometry tracks hidden geometry better than sentence embeddings of the input or of the model's replies, but on average it is the geometry that models share, not the model's own; the model's own geometry crosses an API boundary only through probabilities, partly through next-token probabilities and fully through log-probability vectors. Reading a closed agent through an open model is consistent with these measurements, and in our tests a monitor built that way predicts and prevents closed agents' violations live. The limits: agreement is \GOneAns\ on our first contexts, lower on fresh ones (\GSixAns) and much weaker within one kind of context; an open model's own hidden state is a better stand-in than the answers; individual models deviate from the average sharing; replies detect a change of state sooner; and an attacker who queries a monitor's score still evades it (\cref{app:limits}). An agent can also police itself through its own API: it prevents the violations that its transcript decides, and violations decided by a fact it never checked need a check of the world.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work reads the geometry of a closed model's state from its answers and monitors closed AI agents before they act. The intended effect is safer deployment: in our tests, blocking flagged actions cut the episodes with an executed violation from about 7\% to 0.2\% on two closed agents. We see four risks. (1) A monitor can give false confidence: it catches violations of the policies that it was trained on and can miss a new policy until that policy has labels (\cref{app:limits}). (2) Blocking has a cost: goal completion fell by 1.8 points on GPT and 5.8 points on Claude. (3) An agent or attacker that can query the monitor's score can reword a violating call until it passes; the score should stay hidden from the agent. (4) Reading a model's state from outside could help an attacker to study a closed model. The geometry uses only ordinary answers; exact recovery needs full log-probabilities, which closed APIs do not return, and it recovers distances between states, not weights or private data. The code is released under the MIT license, and the browser tool does not upload the user's logs.

\section{The interrogation state in chat}
\label{app:psi}
On Claude Haiku~5.5, readouts of the interrogation state trained only on open models forecast which harmful requests Claude refuses. Against a three-level moderation verdict the forecast wins (three preregistered tests); against graded moderators it ties (prospective, 923 conversations: 0.873 vs 0.855). Combined with a moderator, it doubles the precision of an alarm for harmful requests that the model answers (0.535 vs 0.274).
