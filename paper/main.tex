\begin{abstract}
Probes on an agent's hidden states can stop its unsafe action before it runs and catch what monitors of its text miss \citep{fayyazi2026cape}, but they need the weights, and the most capable agents are closed. We read a closed agent's state through its own API alone, with no training and no other model, and monitor, forecast and prevent its violations. An API cannot reveal hidden coordinates (a gauge symmetry), but it reveals their geometry: yes/no questions asked in side branches give answer laws whose Fisher--Rao distances are Euclidean in arcsine coordinates, with a known noise. On eight open models this geometry tracks each model's hidden geometry, largely shared across models; through the shared part, Claude Haiku 5.5's own answers agree with its unseen hidden geometry at 0.49 [0.44, 0.55]. At a proposed call, a fixed read-out of eight policy answers ranks violations within each kind of task at AUROC 0.82--0.99 on four open agents, close to a probe on their hidden states, and carries to prompt injection (0.90--0.99) and to closed agents: GPT-6-luna 0.98; Claude Haiku 5.5 0.91 on unapproved restarts, 0.70 on airline writes, 0.48 pooled. Before its call exists, GPT-6-luna's answers rank the coming violation at 0.92 among calls at the same position (preregistered, fresh episodes), and, within each kind of task and tool, better than another model answering in its seat (0.84 vs 0.73; not separable at the same position), as our theory predicts: the forecast needs the agent's own policy. Live, a gate on that state cut its violating episodes from 32\% to 8\%, against 18\% for a gate on position at the same review rate (relative reductions 0.75 vs 0.44, difference 0.31 [0.06, 0.54]); its offline flag rate predicted the 0.75 (the same law missed the position gate). Every result is preregistered unless marked; failures, such as other answerers doing as well or better at the call, are in the paper and the record. We release the tool.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Interpretability reads what a model knows and what it will do from its hidden state \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}, and for agents a probe can stop an unsafe action before it runs \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. These tools need the weights. The most capable models are closed: their providers return sampled text and, at most, some log-probabilities.

\textbf{Why read the state, not the text.} An agent may make a call it is not authorized to make while its visible reasoning does not show it, and when asked, it may say the call is fine: agents that knew a call was unauthorized made it and then denied it \citep{fayyazi2026cape}, and chain-of-thought often leaves out what drives an answer \citep{turpin2023unfaithful,chen2025reasoning}. A check of the call that the operator writes catches only the rules it names, and needs the facts spread through the transcript: the same \texttt{restart} is allowed after an approval three messages earlier and forbidden without one. So we read the state behind the text. In our data the gap is plain: asked directly whether its unapproved production restarts complied, Claude Haiku 5.5 mostly said yes (AUROC 0.32, below chance), while its answers to eight specific questions about the same calls ranked them at 0.91.

\textbf{We make a closed agent grey, with no training and nothing but its API.} Its hidden coordinates cannot be recovered: any reparametrisation of the hidden space that the next layers undo leaves every output unchanged (\cref{prop:gauge}). What survives is geometry. Ask the model yes/no questions in side branches of its conversation (copies of the conversation so far, each with one question appended, sent through its API and discarded; the agent never sees them): in the angles $\vartheta=2\arcsin\sqrt{P(\textsc{yes})}$, Fisher--Rao distance between the answer laws is plain Euclidean distance (\cref{thm:geometry}), a change of variables that needs no training and is estimable from sampled text on any chat API. At an agent's proposed call, its answers to a few such questions are its \emph{observable state} at the decision, and a fixed read-out of them, with no labels, monitors the call before it runs.

\textbf{What we find, as a chain.} \emph{(i) Reconstruct.} An agent's own answers reveal the geometry of its hidden state: on eight open models the answer geometry tracks each model's hidden geometry, better than the input or the replies; part of that geometry is shared across models, and through it a triangulation estimates how well a closed model's answers reconstruct its own unseen hidden geometry (\cref{sec:geom-results}). \emph{(ii) Monitor.} At a proposed call, a fixed read-out of the agent's answers, never fit to labels, ranks its violations nearly as well as a probe trained on its hidden states and, on open agents, better than a holistic reasoning judge; at the call, another model answering the same questions does about as well (better on Claude Haiku 5.5), as the theory predicts, since the transcript then decides the call; before the call, the closed agent's own answers forecast better than another model's (G44); with no label, the answers tell in which settings the read-out works (\cref{sec:results}). Before each call exists, the answers about the agent's next action rank that call among calls at the same step: on GPT-6-luna, preregistered and replicated on fresh episodes (0.92), though not for Claude Haiku 5.5 on $\tau^2$-bench. \emph{(iii) Carry.} With questions written from each benchmark's policy, the read-out carries to prompt injection on AgentDojo (0.90--0.99) and to two closed models: Claude Haiku 5.5 on CAPE-Bench (0.91 on unapproved restarts; 0.48 pooled over its actions) and $\tau^2$-bench (0.70), and GPT-6-luna on CAPE-Bench (0.98). \emph{(iv) Prevent.} The agent's own answer is a live gate: it prevented 82\% of an open agent's violating episodes and 69\% of those that Claude Haiku 5.5's transcript decides (31\% of all); in a later test, offline flag rates predicted the prevention of five agents; and a gate on GPT-6-luna's pre-call state prevented more than a gate on position. All four steps are preregistered.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Theory} (\cref{sec:method}): what an API reveals of a hidden state (geometry, not coordinates); exact coordinates and a cross-fit estimator of distances from sampled answers (unbiased in its expected coordinates); a triangulation that checks a closed model's reconstruction (\cref{prop:triang}); a label-free ceiling that says where a read-out can work (\cref{prop:snrceil}); a state-space model of an agent across steps, identified from unlabelled runs (\cref{prop:iv}).
\item \emph{Evidence} (\cref{sec:geom-results,sec:results}): the answers track hidden geometry on eight open models, and, by triangulation, a closed model's own; at a decision, a fixed read-out of the agent's own answers monitors open agents nearly as well as a probe on their weights and better than a reasoning judge; it carries to three benchmarks and to two closed agents.
\item \emph{Prevention} (\cref{sec:results}): a live gate on the agent's own answer, with effect sizes predicted in advance, and a released tool (\cref{sec:tool}).
\end{enumerate}
Every confirmatory test (G1--G44b for the method; G43 pending, T1--T28 for trained comparisons) was preregistered: its document and code were hash-locked before the data existed, and for the later tests the lock was published first. Tests still running are marked pending in \cref{app:record}. Failed tests and the limits of each claim are in \cref{app:limits,app:record}.

\section{What a model's answers reveal of its hidden state}
\label{sec:geom-results}
The method reads a model only through its answers. This section tests what those answers reveal of the hidden state, with no training: its geometry, and whether that geometry is the model's own or shared across models. \textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (3{,}360 development, 1{,}440 confirmation), and two sets of 1{,}000 fresh contexts that add four new kinds (news, programming, jailbreaks, prompt injections); 88 questions about the message; eight open chat models from six families. Ground truth: each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at a layer chosen on development data. All results are on held-out contexts (\cref{app:details}).

\textbf{The answers track the hidden geometry better than the input or the replies.} On every model, the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input (gains \GOneGain, every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; preregistered, 8 of 8; \cref{tab:geometry}). The model's own replies, the other output an API gives, do little better than the input (\GFiveRep; preregistered). Agreement rises with the number of questions (from 8 to 88: \GOneMore), and answers sampled from the model, 8 per question, keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. Given the kind of context, the answers keep a partial Spearman of 0.46, the input embedding 0.09 (exploratory).

\textbf{The geometry is shared across models.} A model's answers match another model's hidden geometry as well as its own: on 1{,}000 fresh contexts the mean own-model excess is \GSixExcess, inside the preregistered equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ (\cref{fig:geometry}b), and the hidden geometries of different models agree with each other (\GSixHH). What the answers reveal is the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}.

\textbf{A closed model's own hidden geometry} (G2, G34, preregistered). Claude Haiku 5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of its answers give the same geometry (Spearman \GTwoSplit, above the bound of 0.7 fixed in advance), which carries \GTwoText\ beyond the input embedding. Its hidden states are not visible, so we triangulate (\cref{prop:triang}). On the same contexts and questions, with each open model's answers sampled to the closed design, leaving each open model out as if it were closed predicts how well its answers match its own hidden geometry to within \GVtMae\ on average (\GVtInside\ of 8 inside the predicted interval). For Claude Haiku 5.5 the estimate is \GVtClosed, at the level of its direct agreement with open models' hidden geometries (0.45--0.51) and at the bottom of the open range (\GVtOpenRange). It assumes no own-model excess and an open-model loading; over the open loadings' range the interval is [0.32, 0.53]. At an agent's decision, its 8 policy answers measure only a narrow, violation-relevant slice of the decision state (agreement 0.01--0.16 over all pairs; G36).

\section{The grey box at work: monitoring and policing agents through their APIs}
\label{sec:results}
\textbf{Agents and monitors.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised; an environment checker labels each action. Open agents, whose decision states we store: Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B and Muse-Glimmer-30B. Closed agents, through their public APIs with reasoning off: Claude Haiku 5.5 (Anthropic) and GPT-6-luna (OpenAI).

\textbf{The observable state at the decision} (\cref{fig:teaser}c; G15, preregistered). At each proposed call, the agent answers 8 yes/no questions about the call through its own API (4 answers each), and the fixed read-out of \cref{sec:method} scores it; nothing is fit and no label is used (the questions and the read-out were fixed on development data). Within-template AUROC on held-out calls: \GFtFixed; against a probe trained on the agent's true decision states (a trained comparison), \GFtTrainedWB; mean difference \GFtTtwo; against the single self-audit answer, \GFtTone. With no label, the theory also tells which questions carry the signal: across eight agent-benchmark pairs, a question's label-free spread in arcsine coordinates beyond its sampling noise predicts how well it ranks violations (mean Spearman \GTfFone; G25, preregistered). Another model answering the same questions does about as well on open agents (own $-$ other +0.012 [$-$0.018, 0.040], G16) and better on Claude Haiku 5.5 ($-$0.09 on restarts; $-$0.29 on $\tau^2$ with reasoning on, G21): the questions read the geometry that models share (\cref{sec:geom-results}). On the closed model Claude Haiku 5.5 the read-out ranks its unapproved restarts at \GFtClosedAFixed, where its single self-audit answer scores 0.32.

\textbf{Against a reasoning judge} (G20, preregistered, two-sided). Gemma-4 with reasoning on, given each conversation and asked for one holistic violation probability, ranks the open agents' violations worse than the questionnaire (read-out $-$ judge \GTwJone; \cref{tab:g15}): fixed questions and averaged angles beat one verdict. On Claude Haiku 5.5's actions, answered with its reasoning off, the judge is better (\cref{tab:g15}).

\textbf{Prompt injection, a second benchmark} (G17, preregistered; \cref{tab:g15}). On AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, the violation is a call that carries out an instruction hidden in a tool output. With 8 questions written from that policy before any answer existed, the same read-out ranks the open agents' injected calls at \GSvGemma\ (Gemma-4, \GSvGemmaN) and \GSvGpt\ (gpt-oss-20b, \GSvGptN) within suite, on held-out tasks with no defense; registered bar: lower bound above 0.60. Here the single overall question is nearly as good (\GSvGemmaSingle, \GSvGptSingle; difference \GSvVtwo). Claude Haiku 5.5 almost never carried out these injections (no injected call in 271 development episodes), so this benchmark has no closed-agent arm.

\textbf{A second benchmark for the closed agent} (G18, preregistered; \cref{tab:g15}). On $\tau^2$-bench airline \citep{barres2025tau2}, Claude Haiku 5.5 breaks the published policy at some of its writes (for example, it cancels a booking that meets none of the conditions). At each write, Claude Haiku 5.5 answered 8 questions written from the airline policy before any answer existed, and the same fixed read-out scored them. Within-tool AUROC: \GEeRone\ (\GEeN, all in 7 tasks; registered bar: lower bound above 0.5), where its single answer to whether the call complies scores \GEeSingle\ (difference \GEeRtwo). With 5\% of writes reviewed it catches \GEeCaught. Per task (exploratory), it ranks the violations of 5 of the 7 tasks (33 of 50) at 0.77--1.00; in the other two the agent erred and its own reply called the write eligible (a flown date missed; a trip reversed), so its state held no warning to read.

\textbf{A second closed agent, at its call and before it} (G38, preregistered). GPT-6-luna ran CAPE-Bench as an agent (T11). Through its own API, its answers to the same 8 questions rank its violations within template and tool at \GGlAt\ (\GGlN; single answer 0.795). Before the call exists, with the call not shown, its answers about its next action rank the coming violation at \GGlPre. Among calls at the same position, where position cannot help, they rank it at \GGlPrePos\ (exploratory); a preregistered replication on \GCfN\ confirms it: \GCfPos\ (G40). So the state read through a closed agent's API before it acts can carry the coming violation beyond its position. \textbf{Whose state?} Gemma-4, answering the same questions in GPT-6-luna's seat on the same conversations, forecasts worse: 0.728 against 0.840 for GPT-6-luna's own answers (+0.111 [0.041, 0.185]; G44), as \cref{prop:selfforecast} predicts, while at the call other readers match the agent (G16). On open agents it is mixed (Qwen3.8 +0.21; gpt-oss-20b $-$0.16, Muse-Glimmer $-$0.15; G44b). It did not for Claude Haiku 5.5 before its $\tau^2$ writes (0.54 [0.43, 0.68]; G37).

\textbf{The method says where it works} (G32, preregistered). With no label, the read-out's variance beyond its known sampling noise (its SNR, \cref{prop:snrceil}) predicts its AUROC over \GCwCells\ agent-benchmark-stratum cells (Spearman \GCwCone): cells with SNR $>0$ score \GCwPos, cells with SNR $\le0$ \GCwNeg. Claude Haiku 5.5's settings have the lowest SNR: its four answers agree on 96--97\% of questions.

\textbf{The state before the call} (G27, G28, preregistered). Before each step's call, Gemma-4 answered 8 questions about its next action, on 2{,}505 steps of 300 held-out episodes. Their geometry agrees only weakly with the stored decision states (Spearman 0.05--0.07 over all steps, G28; within a step 0.16 on Gemma-4, 0.07 [$-$0.00, 0.14] on three more agents, G30). Among calls at the same step of the same kind of task, the answers rank the violating ones before the call exists: 0.67 on Gemma-4 (exploratory), confirmed on three more agents (0.669 [0.594, 0.764], G30; 0.73 with 16 answers per question, G31). The state has a short memory (autocorrelation 0.13 at one step, 0.01 at three; G31c), and the deciding fact enters at the call's own step (answers then rank it better than one step earlier, +0.16 [0.03, 0.30]; no warning before the first call; G31b). A forecast from earlier steps failed on Gemma-4 (G27), where the filter assumed a noise about three times too large (G31c), but beat position on three more agents (+0.21 [0.08, 0.32]; G27b). A live gate on this pre-call state, against a gate on position and a random gate, is G29b (\cref{app:record}).

\textbf{Self-policing.} Before a non-read call runs, a side branch asks the agent whether the call complies with its policy; a flagged call goes to review. At one state a gate prevents the share $\tau$ of violating calls that the agent flags; over many states, the mean of $\tau$ weighted by where the violations occur (\cref{prop:prevention}). Live, on unseen scenarios (\cref{tab:selfpolice}), Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes and reached more goals; Claude Haiku 5.5, asked to check its policy rule by rule, prevented 69\% of the violating episodes its transcript decides, at no cost in goals. \textbf{A gate on a closed agent's state before the call} (G39b, preregistered; a first design, G39, compared gates of unequal review rates and did not separate them). Live, on GPT-6-luna, at an equal review rate (\GLbRev\ reviews per episode), a gate on its pre-call read-out cut the episodes with a violation from \GLbNone\ to \GLbPre\ (relative reduction \GLbRed), a gate on position to \GLbPos\ (difference \GLbPmP); success was unchanged (\GLbSucc). The offline share of violating calls the gate holds, fixed before the analysis, predicted its effect (\GLwPred, observed \GLwObs; G42), but under-predicted the position gate's (0.17 vs 0.44), whose episodes also reached the goal more often (27.8\% vs 18.7\%). On Gemma-4, whose pre-call read-out ranks weakly, it did not prevent (G29b). \textbf{The effect is predictable} (\cref{fig:teaser}d; preregistered): offline flag rates, weighted by where each agent violates, predicted the live reduction of five agents within the registered 0.15 (from 0.01 to 0.63; mean absolute error 0.035). \textbf{Against prompt injection} (AgentDojo, G13; predictions fixed in advance), the gate cut attack success by 84\% and 50\% at no utility cost.

\section{The tool}
\label{sec:tool}
We release a Python package and a website (links in the camera-ready version). Training-free and API-only: \texttt{graybox.selfaudit} asks an agent the questions of \cref{sec:method} about each proposed call and returns its observable state and the fixed read-out (\texttt{observable\_state}), or holds a call on its own answer (\texttt{SelfAudit}); \texttt{graybox-geometry} measures a chat model's observable geometry and states its cost first. \textbf{Cost.} With Claude Haiku 5.5 as the answerer, the 32 one-word answers per call (8 questions, 4 answers each) cost \$0.0058 per call with prompt caching (G18: 360 calls for \$2.07); an open 26B model on one GPU answered for 360 calls, with reasoning, in 65 minutes (G21). As options that need labels, a user can train a reader monitor in the browser, where the logs stay, and run it in a tool loop, an MCP proxy or an agent framework (\cref{app:tool}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Information geometry and shared representations.} The Fisher metric and its uniqueness are classical \citep{cencov1982statistical,campbell1986extended,amari2016information}, as are the arcsine transform \citep{anscombe1948transformation} and classical scaling \citep{torgerson1952mds}; representational similarity analysis compares distance matrices without a fit \citep{kriegeskorte2008rsa}, and its cross-validated (crossnobis) distance is an unbiased estimator from two independent halves \citep{walther2016reliability}, the same device as our cross-fit. Representations of different models converge \citep{huh2024platonic}. \citet{picozzi2026information} show that the Fisher--Rao geometry of next-token distributions is set by behaviour up to symmetries and shared across architectures; \citet{schiekiera2026associations} compare behavioural and hidden-state geometry in eight models. Our measurement needs only sampled answers, and we test it against hidden states with a cross-model control.
\textbf{Black-box access.} Logits span a $d$-dimensional image \citep{finlayson2024logits}, and a production model's output projection is recovered up to an orthogonal map \citep{carlini2024stealing}; \cref{thm:exact} uses the same structure for the gauge-invariant hidden geometry. Replies can test which model an API serves \citep{gao2025model}. Answers to questions serve as interpretable embeddings \citep{benara2024crafting}, detect lies with follow-up questions \citep{pacchiardi2024liar,cooney2026didyoulie,sam2025quere}, and forecast behaviour \citep{levy2026forecasting}; open readers probe closed transcripts \citep{conti2026hermeneutics}, and an open surrogate's log-probabilities audit a black-box agent's tool calls \citep{zhao2026proxy}; both need a second model, which the grey box does not. We found no prior estimate of how well a closed model's outputs reconstruct its own hidden geometry (\cref{prop:triang}). Decomposing an evaluation into yes/no checklist questions makes LLM judges agree better with people than direct scores do \citep{cook2024tick,lee2024checkeval}; we bring decomposition to monitoring an agent's action before it runs, with a fixed read-out and no labels, and test it against probes on hidden states and a holistic reasoning judge (G15, G20).
\textbf{Probes, monitors and control.} Probes on internals read truth, refusal and deception \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}, and CAPE probes an agent before each action \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. Chain-of-thought monitors catch reward hacking that action-only monitors miss \citep{baker2025monitoring,korbak2025cot,chen2025reasoning,arnav2025redhanded}; \cref{cor:action} delimits when the reasoning adds nothing at the Bayes level; for trained readers on policy labels, it did not help reliably (Qwen3.8 gained $+0.028$) and hurt transfer across agents (G8). An auxiliary open model's hidden states serve as features for a closed model's hallucinations \citep{sriramanan2024llmcheck}, and white-box probes are combined with black-box monitors where activations are available \citep{parrack2025blacktowhite}; we instead read a closed agent through answers to fixed questions, with no training; the answers may be the agent's own or another model's (G16). Agents recognize their own misconduct after the fact \citep{li2025odcv}; deterministic checks of the world before a write recover silent policy violations \citep{reddy2026reasonless}; and resampling suspicious actions limits an adversarial agent \citep{bhatt2025ctrlz}. We show that the agent's own audit, through its API and \emph{before} the action, prevents 82\% and 69\% of the violating episodes that its transcript decides, and offline flag rates predict how much (G12), and confirm the predicted boundary: violations decided by an unchecked fact need such a check of the world. AI control audits a fixed budget of actions \citep{greenblatt2024control}; \cref{prop:frozen,prop:burst} give the budget a label-free, exact and burst-robust form, and the frozen cut's escalation law is the coverage law of split conformal prediction \citep{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2023conformal}. Self-policing is \emph{untrusted monitoring} \citep{greenblatt2024control} without an adversary; it cannot stop a model that colludes with itself. Latents made identifiable by training are recovered by complete encoders \citep{zhou2026identifiabilities}; \cref{prop:gauge} is the output-side counterpart.

\section{Conclusion}
The geometry of a closed agent's hidden state can be reconstructed through its own API, with no training and no other model, and its violations monitored, forecast and prevented from it. The answers' geometry has exact coordinates and a known noise; it tracks open models' hidden geometry, and triangulation checks a closed model's. A fixed read-out ranked open agents' violations nearly as well as a probe on their hidden states; before the call existed, a closed agent's state ranked the coming violation beyond its position; and a gate on it prevented violations by an amount predicted in advance (an earlier law and the position gate's prediction failed; \cref{app:limits}).

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work monitors closed AI agents before they act, through their own APIs and with no training. The intended effect is safer deployment: in our tests, a gate on the agent's own answers prevented 82\% of an open agent's violating episodes and 69\% of those that a closed agent's transcript decides. We see four risks. (1) False confidence: the read-out cannot see a violation decided by a fact the agent never checked, and its questions cover the kinds of violation they were written for; a new policy needs new questions. (2) Holding calls costs time and, for trained gates, task success (1.8 and 5.8 points). (3) An agent that knows the questions, or an attacker who queries a score, may learn to answer them falsely; the questions and scores should stay hidden from the agent, and self-policing is no defense against a model that colludes with itself. (4) Reading a model's state from outside could help an attacker study a closed model; the method uses only ordinary answers and recovers geometry, not weights or private data. The code is released under the MIT license, and the browser tool does not upload the user's logs.

\section{The interrogation state in chat}
\label{app:psi}
On Claude Haiku 5.5, readouts of the interrogation state trained only on open models forecast which harmful requests Claude Haiku 5.5 refuses. Against a three-level moderation verdict the forecast wins (three preregistered tests); against graded moderators it ties (prospective, 923 conversations: 0.873 vs 0.855). Combined with a moderator, it doubles the precision of an alarm for harmful requests that the model answers (0.535 vs 0.274).
