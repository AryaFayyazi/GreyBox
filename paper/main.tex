\begin{abstract}
Probes on hidden states can stop an AI agent's unsafe action before it runs, but they need the weights, and the most capable agents are closed. We make a closed agent grey with no training and nothing but its API. Its hidden coordinates cannot be read from its outputs (a gauge symmetry leaves every output unchanged), but their geometry can: yes/no questions asked in side branches of the conversation give answer laws whose Fisher--Rao distances are Euclidean in arcsine coordinates, estimable without bias from sampled text. On eight open models this geometry tracks the hidden geometry, and it is shared across models, Claude's included. At an agent's proposed call, its answers to eight questions written in advance from the policy are its observable state at the decision, and a fixed read-out of them, with nothing fit to labels, ranks its violations within each kind of task: AUROC 0.82--0.99 on held-out calls of four open agents, close to a probe trained on their hidden states, and 0.91 on the closed Claude Haiku~5.5's unapproved restarts, where its single answer scores 0.32. On a second benchmark, $\tau^2$-bench, it ranks Claude's policy violations at 0.70 [0.54, 0.82]. Violations decided by a fact the agent never checked stay out of reach. Used as a gate, the agent's own answer prevented 82\% of an open agent's violating episodes and 69\% of those Claude's transcript decides, by amounts that a law predicted for five agents (mean error 0.035). Unless marked exploratory, every result is preregistered. We release the tool.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Interpretability reads what a model knows and what it will do from its hidden state \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}, and for agents a probe can stop an unsafe action before it runs \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. These tools need the weights. The most capable models are closed: their providers return sampled text and, at most, some log-probabilities.

\textbf{We make a closed agent grey, with no training and nothing but its API.} Its hidden coordinates cannot be recovered: any reparametrisation of the hidden space that the next layers undo leaves every output unchanged (\cref{prop:gauge}). What survives is geometry. Ask the model yes/no questions in side branches of its conversation: in the angles $\vartheta=2\arcsin\sqrt{P(\textsc{yes})}$, Fisher--Rao distance between the answer laws is plain Euclidean distance (\cref{thm:geometry}), a change of variables that needs no training and is estimable from sampled text on any chat API. At an agent's proposed call, its answers to a few such questions are its \emph{observable state} at the decision, and a fixed read-out of them, with no labels, monitors the call before it runs.

\textbf{What we find.} (i) The observable geometry tracks hidden geometry on eight open models, beyond the kind of context, and it is shared across models, Claude's included (\cref{sec:geom-results}). (ii) At the decision, a fixed read-out of the agent's own eight answers ranks its violations nearly as well as a probe trained on its hidden states (preregistered; a probe tuned afterwards is 0.015 higher), and on a second benchmark it ranks the closed Claude's violations above chance, at 0.70 (preregistered). (iii) Its one-question form, the agent's own answer to whether the call complies, prevents violations live: 82\% on an open agent and 69\% of those Claude's transcript decides; a law predicts the effect for five agents within 0.15, and on a second benchmark it cuts successful prompt injections by 84\% and 50\% (all preregistered).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A training-free grey box} (\cref{sec:method}): what an API can reveal of a hidden state (geometry, not coordinates), exact coordinates for it, an unbiased estimator from sampled text, and a fixed read-out of the observable state at an agent's decision.
\item \emph{Validation against hidden states}: on eight open models and Claude the answers reveal the latent geometry that models share (\cref{sec:geom-results}); at the decision, the agent's answers monitor about as well as a probe trained on its weights (G15), and on a second benchmark for the closed agent (G18).
\item \emph{Prevention through the API}: live self-policing of open and closed agents, a law that predicts it, and a second benchmark (\cref{sec:results}); trained monitors serve as comparisons. We release the tool (\cref{sec:tool}).
\end{enumerate}
Every confirmatory test was preregistered (we name them G1--G18 for the method and T1--T28 for the trained comparisons; \cref{app:record}): its document and analysis code were hash-locked (SHA-256) before the data existed, and for the later tests, all geometry tests included, the lock was published first. Tests that failed, a dropped design, a correction of our own theorem, and the limits of each claim are in \cref{app:limits,app:record}.

\section{What a model's answers reveal of its hidden state}
\label{sec:geom-results}
The method reads a model only through its answers. This section tests what those answers reveal of the hidden state, with no training: its geometry, and whether that geometry is the model's own or shared across models. \textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (3{,}360 development, 1{,}440 confirmation), and two sets of 1{,}000 fresh contexts that add four new kinds (news, programming, jailbreaks, prompt injections); 88 questions about the message; eight open chat models from six families. Ground truth: each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at a layer chosen on development data. All results are on held-out contexts (\cref{app:details}).

\textbf{The answers track the hidden geometry better than embeddings of the input or the replies.} On every model the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input (MiniLM; \cref{tab:geometry}; gains \GOneGain, every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; preregistered, 8 of 8). An embedding of the model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, does little better than that of the input (\GFiveRep; preregistered: \GFiveOut): one reply and the mean of eight differ by at most \GFiveSatPoint\ (upper bound \GFiveSat), while from 8 to 88 questions the answers' agreement rises by \GOneMore. Answers sampled from the model's own probabilities, 8 per question, keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. Three qualifications. Against stronger sentence encoders of the input or of the replies (mpnet; Qwen3-Embedding), the answers stay ahead for \StrongConfirm\ models on these contexts and \StrongFresh\ on fresh ones; for Gemma-3 and gpt-oss a strong embedding matches or beats them (exploratory), and an open model's own hidden state does better still (below). Much of the agreement is the separation of kinds of context: a label of the kind alone reaches a median of 0.57. Given the kind, the answers keep a partial Spearman of 0.46 and the input embedding 0.09 (fresh contexts: 0.43 and 0.09; within one kind, medians \WKAns\ and \WKText; exploratory). And on the fresh contexts of the later tests agreement is lower (answers \GSixAns, input \GSixText, replies \GSixRep); there Gemma's replies beat its answers.

\textbf{On average the geometry is shared, not the model's own.} An independent review asked whether a model's answers match \emph{its own} hidden geometry better than another model's. On average they do not. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the agreement of model A's answers with model B's hidden geometry has no mean own-model excess beyond row and column effects: \GSixExcess, inside the preregistered equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ (\cref{fig:geometry}b). Individual models differ (\GSixPerModel; exploratory), and on the 400 contexts of the new kinds the mean excess is \GSixNewExcess\ (exploratory). The hidden geometries of different models agree with each other (\GSixHH), and an open model's hidden state (Qwen3-8B) matches another model's hidden geometry better than that model's own answers do, for \GSixSTwo\ models (\GSixSTwoRange; preregistered). So what a model's answers reveal of its state is, on average, the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}. \citet{schiekiera2026associations} report behavioural signal beyond cross-model consensus for word-similarity judgements; with our battery we find none on average.

\textbf{The answers beat the input embedding on new kinds of context.} On the 400 contexts of the four new kinds, they do so for \GSixSThree\ models (\GSixAnsNew\ vs \GSixTextNew; preregistered; for Gemma and gpt-oss the interval includes 0).

\textbf{What crosses the API boundary} (G7, preregistered; \cref{app:channels}). Yes/no answers carry little or no model-specific geometry; next-token probabilities carry some, and full log-probability vectors single out a model's own final-layer geometry (\cref{thm:exact}). So what yes/no answers reveal is mostly the geometry that models share, not a model's own.

\textbf{A closed model.} Claude Haiku~5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of the samples give the same geometry: Spearman \GTwoSplit, above the bound of 0.7 fixed in advance from open models, and \GTwoText\ beyond the input embedding (preregistered). Most cells are unanimous, so this stability partly reflects how deterministic Claude is, and with 4 samples per half the estimate is of a contracted transform of the Fisher--Rao geometry (\cref{thm:geometry}d). With the cross-fit estimator the geometry is close to Euclidean (negative eigenvalue mass \CFClaudeNegCF, against \CFClaudeNeg\ with the variance-subtraction rule). Its agreement with the open models' hidden geometries is \GTwoOpen; with the same protocol, open models' answers agree with other open models' hidden geometries at \ClaudeRefOther\ (exploratory). Since answers carry no model-specific part on average, this measures the shared geometry; it says nothing about Claude's own hidden states, which no one outside can check.


\textbf{Where answers lose.} For detecting \emph{that} a model's state moved (a hidden system prompt), sampled replies had more power than sampled answers at equal calls in every model and condition on development data, so we did not run that test (\cref{app:limits}).

\section{The grey box at work: monitoring and policing agents through their APIs}
\label{sec:results}
\textbf{Agents and monitors.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised; an environment checker labels each action. Open agents, whose decision states we store: Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B and Muse-Glimmer-30B. Closed agents, through their public APIs with reasoning off: Claude Haiku~5.5 and GPT-6-luna. The reader is Qwen3-8B (layer 32).

\textbf{The observable state at the decision} (\cref{fig:teaser}c; G15, preregistered). At each proposed call, the agent answers 8 yes/no questions about the call through its own API (4 answers each), and the fixed read-out of \cref{sec:method} scores it; nothing is fit to labels and no label is used (the questions, the read-out and the arcsine coordinates were fixed on development data). Within-template AUROC on held-out calls: \GFtFixed; against a probe trained on the agent's true decision states (a trained comparison), \GFtTrainedWB; mean difference \GFtTtwo; against the single self-audit answer, \GFtTone. A probe tuned afterwards on the same development calls (exploratory) scores 0.991, 0.841, 0.950 and 0.972: 0.015 above the read-out on average, and 0.043 above it on Muse-Glimmer. On the closed Claude Haiku~5.5 the read-out ranks its unapproved restarts at \GFtClaudeFixed, where its single self-audit answer scores 0.32; these restarts are easy for any reader of the command text (a trained reader scores 1.000), so this is a floor that the fixed read-out nearly reaches, not a hard case. A trained comparison that recreates the decision state from an open reader and the answers, and reads it with a probe trained on true states, scores \GFtGrey\ (G14, preregistered, \cref{app:greybox}); there the answers add nothing to the reader (\GFtPthree), so the trained pipeline's gain comes from labels learned on open agents.

\textbf{A second benchmark for the closed agent} (G18, preregistered; \cref{tab:g15}). On $\tau^2$-bench airline \citep{barres2025tau2}, Claude Haiku~5.5 breaks the published policy at some of its writes (for example, it cancels a booking that meets none of the conditions). At each write, Claude answered 8 questions written from the airline policy before any answer existed, and the same fixed read-out scored them. Within-tool AUROC: \GEeRone\ (\GEeN, all in 7 tasks; registered bar: lower bound above 0.5), where its single answer to whether the call complies scores \GEeSingle\ (difference \GEeRtwo). With 5\% of writes reviewed it catches \GEeCaught. On the writes that a zero-shot reasoning judge (Qwen3-32B, thinking on) also scored, the read-out scores \GEeJudgeOurs\ and the judge \GEeJudge\ (\GEeRthree; 224 writes and 17 violations, all from the first two runs; the interval includes 0). The read-out is weaker within one task (0.61 [0.46, 0.97], exploratory): it marks situations prone to violation more than single writes. In $\tau^2$ the user turn is the customer's, so a fixed note in the system prompt says that the check comes from the operator; without it, Claude declined to answer. \textbf{The position does not matter here} (G19, preregistered): the same model, reading the same conversation as an outside reviewer, scores \GNnOut\ on 180 of these writes, against \GNnOwn\ in its own conversation (\GNnDiff; no detectable difference). As the shared geometry of \cref{sec:geom-results} suggests, the value lies in the questions and the read-out, asked through the API, not in the agent's own seat.

\textbf{Self-policing: the agent's own API as the monitor.} Self-policing uses the agent's own answer as a gate. Before a non-read call runs, we ask the agent in a side branch, which its conversation never sees, whether the call complies with its policy; a flagged call goes to review (\emph{gate}). At one state, a gate prevents the share $\tau$ of violating calls that the audit flags (\cref{prop:prevention}, \cref{app:agents}); over many states it prevents the mean of $\tau$ weighted by where the violations occur. On unseen scenarios (\cref{tab:selfpolice}), Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes (186 to 33 of 666) and reached more goals; the reviewer was CAPE's checker, a perfect stand-in for a person, so a held allowed call cost nothing but a review (1.01 per episode). Claude Haiku~5.5 needed the policy checked rule by rule (asked only whether a call complies, it flagged 5\% of its violations); so audited, it prevented 69\% of the violating episodes its transcript decides (26 to 8 of 940, all production restarts), at no cost in goals. It did not prevent test sends to a mailing list whose external members appear only after sending (7\% [0, 17]; as locked): a judge with no prior over that unseen fact cannot flag them, while the reader gate's learned prior does. \textbf{The effect is predictable} (\cref{fig:teaser}d, \cref{fig:law}; preregistered). Each agent's offline flag rates per kind of task, weighted by where it violates (from the plain arm, as registered), predicted the live reduction of five agents (on G12's scenario set; Gemma-4's 0.63 there differs from G9's 0.82 on another set) within the registered 0.15 (set after retrospective fits had missed by up to 0.11), from 0.01 (Qwen3.8) to 0.63 (Gemma-4), Claude included; mean absolute error 0.035. The law over-predicts Claude by 0.08 [0.03, 0.13]. With flag rates from another mix of tasks the prediction failed (G11b). \textbf{A second benchmark} (G13, \cref{tab:selfpolice}; predictions fixed in advance): against prompt injection the audit cut attack success by 84\% and 50\% at no utility cost. gpt-oss matched its prediction; Gemma-4 missed its 1.00 by 0.16, mostly through flagged calls that our stand-in reviewer let run.

\textbf{Trained comparisons} (\cref{app:trained}). The rest of this section reports monitors that are \emph{trained}, as comparisons for the training-free method. With labels, an open reader of the transcript is a monitor: a logistic probe on its state, trained only on 17{,}623 Gemma-4 steps, scores every agent's actions (\cref{tab:main}); baselines are the agent's own internals (where open), TF-IDF with the same labels, a zero-shot 70B judge, a reasoning judge given the policy, and the action's tool alone. \textbf{The transcript is enough.} \Cref{cor:action} says that the agent's internals cannot beat a monitor of the transcript and the action. A probe on Gemma-4's own internals scores 0.841 at the start of the turn and 0.979 after its reasoning; the reader probe, trained on the same labels, scores 0.994 ($+0.015$ [0.007, 0.026] over the internals) and is non-inferior on unseen task families (\cref{tab:main}). Given the action, adding the agent's hidden reasoning changes Gemma-4's AUROC by $-0.0014$ [$-0.0046$, $+0.0014$]. On three other reasoning agents (G8, preregistered) the reasoning did not help reliably (Qwen3.8 gains \GEightQwen{}) and lowered transfer from Gemma-4 by \GEightTransfer{} (\cref{app:limits}). Fit on the agent's own steps, the reader's state replicates 46--66\% of the variance of the agent's top 128 decision-state components on four architectures (\cref{app:agents,app:rb}). Over word counts it adds robustness to shift: TF-IDF ties it in distribution but is at chance on AgentDojo (0.503 vs 0.781).

\textbf{Two closed agents.} Nothing was fit on the closed agents. At a cut set at the top 5\% of the agent's own unlabelled traffic, the monitor catches 94.1\% [85.8, 97.7] of Claude Haiku~5.5's 68 violations (zero-shot judge: 0\%; TF-IDF: 63.8\%) and 98.5\% [91.9, 99.7] of GPT-6-luna's (judge 37.9\%; TF-IDF 49.0\%; Wilson intervals). Claude's violations are two behaviours: 42 test sends to a mailing list with thousands of external members it never checked, and 26 production restarts without approval. A prior that knows only each template's and tool's violation rate, fit out of fold, catches 91.6\% [82.1, 95.9] (AUROC 0.992; reader 0.989), so on Claude the result shows that the monitor flags these two behaviours, not that it reads the deciding facts. GPT-6-luna's 66 violations span six templates, each with safe calls, and there the monitor beats that prior (98.5\% vs 86.4\% [76.1, 92.7]), as on Gemma-4 (\cref{app:limits}). Score scales do not transfer between agents (a cut at 0.5 catches 3\% on Claude), rankings do: the cut must come from the agent's own traffic. Frozen after 500 actions, it keeps catching 94\% under bursts in which half the actions violate; a cut that moves over 5{,}000 actions falls to 36\% (\cref{prop:burst}, \cref{fig:transfer}a).

\textbf{Live prevention.} We ran the monitor as a server in each closed agent's tool loop, on the same scenarios with and without the gate. In all four preregistered live tests the gate removed nearly all executed violations (\cref{tab:live,fig:prevention}), and no agent executed a violation after a block. Holding an escalated action for a reviewer kept the safety gain; its preregistered bound on the goal cost was not shown ($-0.020$ [$-0.051$, $+0.007$]; \cref{app:limits}). The round trip was \LATENCY.



\textbf{An agent that knows it is monitored, and an attacker who queries the score.} Told that a monitor reviews every call, Claude violated as often; the monitor caught 97.1\% at the frozen cut, which then escalated 15.2\% of actions (88.6\% at a 5\% budget; preregistered); a cut set again on its unlabelled traffic removed every executed violation live (\cref{tab:live}). An attacker who queries the score and rewrites a violating call's free text lowered the catch from 96\% to 29\%, and two preregistered defenses failed (\cref{app:limits}): the score must stay hidden from the agent.

\textbf{Labels of a new policy transfer from open agents.} Prompt injection on AgentDojo is absent from our training data; its labels from two open agents transfer to the closed GPT-6-luna (AUROC 0.922 vs the judge's 0.787; \cref{tab:main}). Here the limit was the coverage of policies, not the agent; on $\tau^2$-bench, with test tasks unseen, the reader fell to 0.54 (\cref{app:limits}).

\section{The tool}
\label{sec:tool}
We release a Python package and a website (links in the camera-ready version). Training-free and API-only: \texttt{graybox.selfaudit} asks an agent the questions of \cref{sec:method} about each proposed call and returns its observable state and the fixed read-out (\texttt{observable\_state}), or holds a call on its own answer (\texttt{SelfAudit}); \texttt{graybox-geometry} measures a chat model's observable geometry and states its cost first. As options that need labels, a user can train a reader monitor in the browser, where the logs stay, and run it in a tool loop, an MCP proxy or an agent framework (\cref{app:tool}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Information geometry and shared representations.} The Fisher metric and its uniqueness are classical \citep{cencov1982statistical,campbell1986extended,amari2016information}, as are the arcsine transform \citep{anscombe1948transformation} and classical scaling \citep{torgerson1952mds}; representational similarity analysis compares distance matrices without a fit \citep{kriegeskorte2008rsa}, and its cross-validated (crossnobis) distance is an unbiased estimator from two independent halves \citep{walther2016reliability}, the same device as our cross-fit. Representations of different models converge \citep{huh2024platonic}. \citet{picozzi2026information} show that the Fisher--Rao geometry of next-token distributions is set by behaviour up to symmetries and shared across architectures; \citet{schiekiera2026associations} compare behavioural and hidden-state geometry in eight models. Our measurement needs only sampled answers, and we test it against hidden states with a cross-model control.
\textbf{Black-box access.} Logits span a $d$-dimensional image \citep{finlayson2024logits}, and a production model's output projection is recovered up to an orthogonal map \citep{carlini2024stealing}; \cref{thm:exact} uses the same structure for the gauge-invariant hidden geometry. Replies can test which model an API serves \citep{gao2025model}. Answers to questions serve as interpretable embeddings \citep{benara2024crafting}, detect lies with follow-up questions \citep{pacchiardi2024liar,cooney2026didyoulie,sam2025quere}, and forecast behaviour \citep{levy2026forecasting}; open readers probe closed transcripts \citep{conti2026hermeneutics}.
\textbf{Probes, monitors and control.} Probes on internals read truth, refusal and deception \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}, and CAPE probes an agent before each action \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. Chain-of-thought monitors catch reward hacking that action-only monitors miss \citep{baker2025monitoring,korbak2025cot,chen2025reasoning,arnav2025redhanded}; \cref{cor:action} delimits when the reasoning adds nothing at the Bayes level; for trained readers on policy labels, it did not help reliably (Qwen3.8 gained $+0.028$) and hurt transfer across agents (G8). An auxiliary open model's hidden states serve as features for a closed model's hallucinations \citep{sriramanan2024llmcheck}, and white-box probes are combined with black-box monitors where activations are available \citep{parrack2025blacktowhite}; we instead read a closed agent through its own answers, with no auxiliary model and no training. Agents recognize their own misconduct after the fact \citep{li2025odcv}; deterministic checks of the world before a write recover silent policy violations \citep{reddy2026reasonless}; and resampling suspicious actions limits an adversarial agent \citep{bhatt2025ctrlz}. We show that the agent's own audit, through its API and \emph{before} the action, prevents the violations that its transcript decides, by an amount that a law predicts (G12), and confirm the predicted boundary: violations decided by an unchecked fact need such a check of the world. AI control audits a fixed budget of actions \citep{greenblatt2024control}; \cref{prop:frozen,prop:burst} give the budget a label-free, exact and burst-robust form, and the frozen cut's escalation law is the coverage law of split conformal prediction \citep{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2023conformal}. Self-policing is \emph{untrusted monitoring} \citep{greenblatt2024control} without an adversary; it cannot stop a model that colludes with itself. Latents made identifiable by training are recovered by complete encoders \citep{zhou2026identifiabilities}; \cref{prop:gauge} is the output-side counterpart.

\section{Conclusion}
A closed agent can be monitored with no training and nothing but its API. Its hidden coordinates cannot be read from its outputs, but the geometry its answers reveal has exact coordinates and is shared across models. At a proposed call, a fixed read-out of the agent's answers to a few questions ranked its violations, within each kind of task, nearly as well as a probe trained on its hidden states, and on a second benchmark it ranked the closed agent's violations above chance, mostly by situation; used as a gate, the agent's own answer prevented the violations its transcript decides, by an amount a law predicts. It cannot see a violation decided by a fact the agent never checked. Limits are in \cref{app:limits}.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work monitors closed AI agents before they act, through their own APIs and with no training. The intended effect is safer deployment: in our tests, a gate on the agent's own answers prevented most of the violations its transcript decides. We see four risks. (1) False confidence: the read-out cannot see a violation decided by a fact the agent never checked, and its questions cover the kinds of violation they were written for; a new policy needs new questions. (2) Holding calls costs time and, for trained gates, task success (1.8 and 5.8 points). (3) An agent that knows the questions, or an attacker who queries a score, may learn to answer them falsely; the questions and scores should stay hidden from the agent, and self-policing is no defense against a model that colludes with itself. (4) Reading a model's state from outside could help an attacker study a closed model; the method uses only ordinary answers and recovers geometry, not weights or private data. The code is released under the MIT license, and the browser tool does not upload the user's logs.

\section{The interrogation state in chat}
\label{app:psi}
On Claude Haiku~5.5, readouts of the interrogation state trained only on open models forecast which harmful requests Claude refuses. Against a three-level moderation verdict the forecast wins (three preregistered tests); against graded moderators it ties (prospective, 923 conversations: 0.873 vs 0.855). Combined with a moderator, it doubles the precision of an alarm for harmful requests that the model answers (0.535 vs 0.274).
