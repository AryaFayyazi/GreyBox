\begin{abstract}
Probes on an agent's hidden states can stop an unsafe action before it runs and catch what monitors of its text miss \citep{fayyazi2026cape}, but they need the weights, and the most capable agents are closed. We ask what a closed agent's own API reveals of its state, with no training and no other model, and what this buys for safety. An API cannot reveal hidden coordinates (a gauge symmetry), but it reveals geometry: yes/no questions asked in throw-away copies of the conversation (side branches) give answer laws whose Fisher--Rao distances are exact Euclidean distances in arcsine coordinates, with a known noise. On eight open models this geometry tracks each model's hidden geometry better than the input or the replies do, and it is the geometry that models share. Three consequences follow, and we test each in preregistered experiments. \emph{At a proposed call}, the transcript decides the violation, so any capable answerer reads it: a fixed read-out of the agent's own answers ranks violations at AUROC 0.82--0.99 on four open agents, close to a probe on their hidden states, 0.90--0.99 on prompt injection and 0.98 on GPT-6-luna, and another model's answers do as well. \emph{Before the call}, the forecast is the agent's own policy: GPT-6-luna's answers about its next action rank the coming violation among calls at the same step at 0.84 and 0.92 on two fresh sets of episodes, and the theory predicts how much any other reader loses. \emph{Live}, gates on these reads prevent violations by amounts that offline flag rates predict; a gate on GPT-6-luna's pre-call state cut its violating episodes from 32\% to 8\%, against 18\% for a gate on the call's step alone at the same review rate. Every test is preregistered, and we release the tool.
\end{abstract}

\section{The tool}
\label{sec:tool}
We release a Python package and a website (links in the camera-ready version). For any agent behind a chat API, the package returns the observable state and the fixed read-out of each proposed call, holds a call on the agent's own answers, and measures a model's answer geometry. On Claude Haiku 5.5, the 32 one-word answers per call cost \$0.0058 with prompt caching (\cref{app:tool}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Information geometry and shared representations.} The Fisher metric and its uniqueness are classical \citep{cencov1982statistical,campbell1986extended,amari2016information}, as are the arcsine transform \citep{anscombe1948transformation} and classical scaling \citep{torgerson1952mds}; representational similarity analysis compares distance matrices without a fit \citep{kriegeskorte2008rsa}, and crossnobis distances use the same cross-fit as ours \citep{walther2016reliability}. Representations of different models converge \citep{huh2024platonic}. \citet{picozzi2026information} show that the Fisher--Rao geometry of next-token distributions is set by behaviour up to symmetries and shared across architectures; \citet{schiekiera2026associations} compare behavioural and hidden-state geometry in eight models.

\textbf{Black-box access.} Logits span a $d$-dimensional image \citep{finlayson2024logits}, and a production model's output projection is recovered up to an orthogonal map \citep{carlini2024stealing}; \cref{thm:exact} uses the same structure for the gauge-invariant hidden geometry. Answers to questions serve as interpretable embeddings \citep{benara2024crafting}, detect lies with follow-up questions \citep{pacchiardi2024liar,cooney2026didyoulie,sam2025quere}, and forecast behaviour \citep{levy2026forecasting}; open readers probe closed transcripts \citep{conti2026hermeneutics}, and an open surrogate's log-probabilities audit a black-box agent's tool calls \citep{zhao2026proxy}; both need a second model. We found no prior estimate of how well a closed model's outputs reconstruct its own hidden geometry (\cref{prop:triang}).

\textbf{Questions instead of one verdict.} Decomposing an evaluation into yes/no checklist questions makes LLM judges agree better with people than direct scores do \citep{cook2024tick,lee2024checkeval}; we bring decomposition to monitoring an agent's action before it runs, with a fixed read-out and no labels, and test it against probes on hidden states and a holistic reasoning judge.

\textbf{Probes and monitors.} Probes on internals read truth, refusal and deception \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}, and CAPE probes an agent before each action \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. Chain-of-thought monitors catch reward hacking that action-only monitors miss \citep{baker2025monitoring,korbak2025cot,chen2025reasoning,arnav2025redhanded}; \cref{cor:action} delimits when the reasoning adds nothing at the Bayes level. An auxiliary open model's hidden states serve as features for a closed model's hallucinations \citep{sriramanan2024llmcheck}, and white-box probes are combined with black-box monitors where activations are available \citep{parrack2025blacktowhite}; we instead read a closed agent through answers to fixed questions, with no training; at the call the answers may be the agent's own or another model's, but before the call the forecast needs the agent's own (\cref{prop:selfforecast}).

\textbf{Self-policing and control.} Agents recognize their own misconduct after the fact \citep{li2025odcv}; deterministic checks of the world before a write recover silent policy violations \citep{reddy2026reasonless}; and resampling suspicious actions limits an adversarial agent \citep{bhatt2025ctrlz}. We show that the agent's own audit, through its API and \emph{before} the action, prevents 82\% and 69\% of the violating episodes that its transcript decides, and offline flag rates predict how much, and confirm the predicted boundary: violations decided by an unchecked fact need such a check of the world. AI control audits a fixed budget of actions \citep{greenblatt2024control}; our frozen cut is split conformal prediction \citep{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2023conformal}. Self-policing is \emph{untrusted monitoring} \citep{greenblatt2024control} without an adversary; it cannot stop a model that colludes with itself.

\section{Conclusion}
A closed agent's API reveals the geometry of its state, not its coordinates, and the geometry it reveals is the one that models share. This one finding sorts what a grey box can do. At a proposed call, the transcript decides the violation, so a fixed read-out of the agent's own answers monitors it with no training, and so can any capable answerer. Before the call, the agent's own policy carries the forecast; on GPT-6-luna its answers about its next action forecast beyond the call's step on two fresh sets of episodes, and a gate on them prevented more than a gate on the step alone, by an amount predicted in advance. Where the theory's label-free noise says a read-out cannot work, or a fact the agent never checked decides the violation, it did not.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work monitors closed AI agents before they act, through their own APIs and with no training. The intended effect is safer deployment: in our tests, a gate on the agent's own answers prevented 82\% of an open agent's violating episodes and 69\% of those that a closed agent's transcript decides. We see four risks. (1) False confidence: the read-out cannot see a violation decided by a fact the agent never checked, and its questions cover the kinds of violation they were written for; a new policy needs new questions. (2) Holding calls costs time and, for trained gates, task success (1.8 and 5.8 points). (3) An agent that knows the questions, or an attacker who queries a score, may learn to answer them falsely; the questions and scores should stay hidden from the agent, and self-policing is no defense against a model that colludes with itself. (4) Reading a model's state from outside could help an attacker study a closed model; the method uses only ordinary answers and recovers geometry, not weights or private data. The code is released under the MIT license, and the browser tool does not upload the user's logs.

\section{The released tool}
\label{app:tool}
The website's browser trainer imports agent logs, lists the riskiest unlabelled action first for review, and trains a reader monitor in the browser, so the logs never leave the user's machine; the monitor then runs in a tool loop, an MCP proxy or an agent framework.

\section{Smaller readers}
\label{app:small}
This appendix tests readers smaller than the shipped one, including encoders that run in the browser. All readers are trained on the same 17{,}623 Gemma-4 CAPE-Bench steps (1{,}339 violations); none is trained on a test set. Browser encoders: all-MiniLM-L6-v2, all-mpnet-base-v2 (three 1{,}000-character views) and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (one instruction-prefixed text, last-token pooling), each with a standardised logistic probe. Decoder readers: Qwen3-0.6B, 1.7B, 4B and 14B at the shipped reader's relative depth, with the shipped probe. The selected browser encoder, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, beats TF-IDF on the closed agent's AgentDojo calls (0.605 vs 0.335; $+0.270$ [0.134, 0.451]), so the website offers it for new kinds of tasks. In the browser it runs as a float16 ONNX model whose features match PyTorch's (cosine 1.000 on 40 steps); the 8-bit model's do not (0.852).

\section{The observable state in chat}
\label{app:psi}
On Claude Haiku 5.5, readouts of the observable state trained only on open models forecast which harmful requests Claude Haiku 5.5 refuses (\cref{fig:psi}). Against a three-level moderation verdict the forecast wins (three preregistered tests). Combined with a moderator, it doubles the precision of an alarm for harmful requests that the model answers (0.535 vs 0.274).
