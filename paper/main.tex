\begin{abstract}
Probes on an agent's hidden states can stop an unsafe action before it runs, but they need the weights, and the most capable agents are closed; their words are no substitute, since agents that violate a policy often deny it. We give a training-free estimator of a closed agent's hidden-state geometry from its API alone, validate it against true hidden states, and use it to forecast and prevent violations. No API reveals hidden coordinates (a gauge symmetry), but their geometry is identifiable: yes/no answers sampled in throw-away copies of the conversation give exact Fisher--Rao coordinates. On eight open models it tracks the hidden geometry (Spearman 0.40--0.74); sampled answers reveal the part models share, log-probabilities the private part. On four open agents, the 8 policy answers are linearly predictable from the decision state across scenarios (0.36--0.60), while their geometry agrees with the full state at only 0.01--0.16. Read on the agent's sampled next actions, this geometry forecasts violations with no label and no question sign on Claude Haiku 5.5 (0.89; 0.87 on fresh episodes), weakly on GPT-6-luna (0.64), and on average over four open agents (0.75; 3 of 4 above chance). Claude's own words do not (GPT-6-luna's do): before 93\% of its violating calls, none of its 32 answers admitted a violation. A probe after the agent's private reasoning forecasts better (by 0.16); at the turn's start, where an API forecasts from, the signed API forecast matched a white-box probe (0.83 vs 0.83; the label-free geometry, 0.81, fell just short). Live, on Claude's one task family with varying violations and with no human, a gate on the estimated geometry alone (no label, no question sign) left 20\% of episodes violating, against 32\% for random holds at the same dose and 66\% with no gate (a first version, with random tie-breaking, did not beat them). We report every preregistered test, passed or failed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Geometry from outputs.} The Fisher metric, the arcsine transform and classical scaling are classical \citep{cencov1982statistical,amari2016information,anscombe1948transformation,torgerson1952mds}. Representations and the Fisher--Rao geometry of next-token laws converge across models \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}, and behavioural similarity tracks hidden similarity on open models \citep{schiekiera2026associations}; logits recover the output projection up to an orthogonal map \citep{finlayson2024logits,carlini2024stealing}, and sampled text identifies a model \citep{pasquini2024llmmap,gao2024model}. None of these estimates hidden geometry from sampled answers alone and checks it against true hidden states; we do.

\textbf{Reading models; monitoring agents.} Answers serve as embeddings \citep{benara2024crafting}, detect lies \citep{pacchiardi2024liar,cooney2026didyoulie} and beat white-box probes on open models \citep{sam2025quere}; open models can monitor closed agents \citep{conti2026hermeneutics,zhao2026proxy}. Self-reports are generic \citep{blandfort2026strangers} and lose to probes on tool calls \citep{advani2026reading,binder2025looking}; agents deny violations \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. Chain-of-thought and hybrid monitors catch misbehaviour \citep{baker2025monitoring,korbak2025cot,arnav2025redhanded,parrack2025blacktowhite}; Ctrl-Z and retrying resample or block suspicious actions \citep{bhatt2025ctrlz,lucassen2026retrying}, while we sample to forecast.

\section{Conclusion}
With no training, a closed agent's hidden-state geometry can be estimated from its API; on Claude it forecasts and gates violations it does not admit.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work monitors closed AI agents before they act, through their own APIs and with no training. The intended effect is safer deployment: in our tests, with CAPE's checker reviewing the held calls, a gate on the agent's own answers prevented 82\% of an open agent's violating episodes and, for a closed agent, 69\% of those that its transcript decides (31\% over all scenarios). We see four risks. (1) False confidence: the read-out cannot see a violation decided by a fact the agent never checked, and its questions cover the kinds of violation they were written for; a new policy needs new questions. (2) Holding calls costs time, and a held allowed call can end an episode early (GPT-6-luna's success changed by $-$0.016 [$-$0.056, 0.028]). (3) An agent that knows the questions, or an attacker who queries a score, may learn to answer them falsely; the questions and scores should stay hidden from the agent, and self-policing is no defense against a model that colludes with itself. (4) Reading a model's state from outside could help an attacker study a closed model; the method uses only ordinary answers and recovers geometry, not weights or private data. The code is released under the MIT license, and the browser tool does not upload the user's logs.

\section{The released tool}
\label{app:tool}
The package \texttt{graybox} runs the method of this paper: it asks a closed agent's API the fixed questions in side branches, computes the angles, the read-out, the geometric monitor and the policy forecast, and holds a flagged call, with no training. As an option, and only as a comparison, a website trains a reader monitor in the browser from the user's labelled logs, so the logs never leave the user's machine. Either monitor runs in a tool loop, an MCP proxy or an agent framework.

\section{Trained comparisons (not the method): smaller readers}
\label{app:small}
This appendix tests readers smaller than the shipped one, including encoders that run in the browser. All readers are trained on the same 17{,}623 Gemma-4 CAPE-Bench steps (1{,}339 violations); none is trained on a test set. Browser encoders: all-MiniLM-L6-v2, all-mpnet-base-v2 (three 1{,}000-character views) and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (one instruction-prefixed text, last-token pooling), each with a standardised logistic probe. Decoder readers: Qwen3-0.6B, 1.7B, 4B and 14B at the shipped reader's relative depth, with the shipped probe. The selected browser encoder, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, beats TF-IDF on the closed agent's AgentDojo calls (0.605 vs 0.335; $+0.270$ [0.134, 0.451]), so the website offers it for new kinds of tasks. In the browser it runs as a float16 ONNX model whose features match PyTorch's (cosine 1.000 on 40 steps); the 8-bit model's do not (0.852).

\section{Trained comparisons (not the method): the observable state in chat}
\label{app:psi}

Against a three-level moderation verdict the forecast wins (three preregistered tests).
