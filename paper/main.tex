\begin{abstract}
The hidden coordinates of a closed model cannot be read from its outputs: a gauge symmetry of the network leaves every output unchanged. What can be read is a geometry. Asking yes/no questions in side branches of the conversation gives answer laws whose Fisher--Rao distances are exactly Euclidean in arcsine coordinates, and a cross-fit estimator is unbiased from sampled text alone. We measure this observable geometry on eight open models and test it against their hidden states, with every test preregistered and its lock published before the data. It tracks the hidden geometry (Spearman \GOneAns) far better than the input text (\GOneText) or the model's own replies (\GFiveRep). But it is not the model's own: on 1{,}000 fresh contexts, a model's answers match other models' hidden geometries as well as its own (own-model excess $+0.002$), on new kinds of context too, and another open model's hidden state is the better stand-in. What a closed model's answers reveal is a latent geometry it shares with open models; Claude Haiku~5.5 reveals the same. What crosses an API boundary depends on what it returns: yes/no answers reveal no model-specific geometry, each returned token probability reveals some, and log-probability vectors single out a model's own internals among eight models for seven. This is a measured reason why an open model can monitor a closed agent: reading transcripts through an open model, a monitor trained only on open agents catches 94\% and 98\% of two closed agents' violations at a 5\% label-free review budget, and cuts the episodes with an executed violation from about 7\% to 0.2\% live. The model-specific final geometry is recoverable exactly only with full log-probabilities. We release the tool.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Interpretability reads what a model knows and what it will do from its hidden state \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}, and for agents a probe can stop an unsafe action before it runs \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. These tools need the weights. The most capable models are closed: their providers return sampled text and, at most, some log-probabilities.

\textbf{What can an outside observer know about a closed model's state?} Not its coordinates: any reparametrisation of the hidden space that the next layers undo leaves every output unchanged, a gauge symmetry (\cref{prop:gauge}). Distances between states survive this symmetry, so we ask which geometry the outputs reveal. Ask the model yes/no questions about a context, each in its own side branch. In the angles $\vartheta=2\arcsin\sqrt{P(\textsc{yes})}$, the Fisher--Rao distance between two contexts' answer laws is plain Euclidean distance (\cref{thm:geometry}): a change of variables, not a learned embedding, and estimable without bias from sampled text on any chat API.

\textbf{What we find.} This observable geometry tracks a model's hidden geometry far better than the input text or the model's own replies. But a control that an independent review asked for shows that it is not the model's own: a model's answers match \emph{other} models' hidden geometries exactly as well as its own, on fresh contexts and new kinds of context, and an open model's hidden state is a better stand-in than the model's own answers. What outputs reveal is the latent geometry that models share \citep{picozzi2026information,huh2024platonic}. A closed model, Claude, reveals the same. This turns a common assumption into a measurement: an open model that reads the same tokens is a principled stand-in for a closed one. We use it to monitor closed agents, and we show that the model-specific part of the geometry needs full log-probabilities (\cref{thm:exact}).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Theory} (\cref{sec:method}): the observable geometry of a model's state in exact flat coordinates, its invariance, its link to the hidden state, and an exactly unbiased estimator from sampled answers; the exact final geometry from full log-probabilities, after \citet{carlini2024stealing}.
\item \emph{Measurement} (\cref{sec:geom-results}): on eight open models, the answer geometry tracks hidden geometry better than the input text and the model's replies; it is shared across models, with no own-model excess, on fresh and new kinds of context; Claude reveals the same geometry; and the model's own geometry crosses the API boundary only through probabilities, more with each returned token. We report where the answers lose.
\item \emph{Application to closed agents} (\cref{sec:results}): with the transcript and the action sufficient for policy checks (\cref{cor:action}), a monitor that reads them through an open model predicts and prevents two closed agents' violations live, also when the agent knows it is monitored.
\item \emph{A released tool} (\cref{sec:tool}): one command measures a model's observable geometry; an SDK, a server and a browser trainer monitor agents.
\end{enumerate}
Every confirmatory test was preregistered: its document and analysis code were hash-locked (SHA-256) before the data existed, and for the later tests, all geometry tests included, the lock was published first. Tests that failed, a dropped design, a correction of our own theorem, and the limits of each claim are in \cref{app:limits,app:record}.

\section{What a model's answers reveal: results}
\label{sec:geom-results}
\textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (harmful and benign requests, reviews, yes/no questions, maths problems, encyclopedia text), split into 3{,}360 development and 1{,}440 confirmation contexts, and 1{,}000 fresh contexts for the last test, 400 of them of four kinds never used before (news, programming tasks, jailbreak behaviours, prompt injections). A battery of 88 questions about the message (topic, intent, tone, demands, harm, and the model's own stance). Eight open chat models from six families. Ground truth: each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at the layer chosen on development contexts only. All results are on held-out contexts, as preregistered.

\textbf{The answers track the hidden geometry; the input text and the replies do not.} On every model the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than the geometry of the input text (\cref{tab:geometry}; gains \GOneGain, every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; preregistered, 8 of 8). The model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, do little better than the text (\GFiveRep; preregistered: \GFiveOut): one reply and the mean of eight differ by at most \GFiveSat, while from 8 to 88 questions the answers' agreement rises by \GOneMore. Answers sampled from the model's own probabilities, 8 per question, keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. Two qualifications. Much of the agreement is the separation of kinds of context: within one kind it is weaker (median \WKAns\ for answers, \WKText\ for text; answers ahead in \WKCells\ model--kind cells; exploratory). And on the fresh contexts of the last test agreement is lower (answers \GSixAns, text \GSixText, replies \GSixRep); there Gemma's replies beat its answers.

\textbf{The geometry is shared, not the model's own.} An independent review asked whether a model's answers match \emph{its own} hidden geometry better than another model's. They do not. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the agreement of model A's answers with model B's hidden geometry has no own-model excess beyond row and column effects: \GSixExcess, inside the preregistered equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ (\cref{fig:geometry}d); the same held on the earlier contexts and in every category of question, including the questions about the model's own stance (exploratory). The hidden geometries of different models agree with each other (\GSixHH), and an open model's hidden state (Qwen3-8B) matches another model's hidden geometry better than that model's own answers do, for \GSixSTwo\ models (\GSixSTwoRange; preregistered). So what a model's outputs reveal of its state is the geometry that models share, as \citet{picozzi2026information} found for next-token distributions and in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic}; the model-specific remainder of the hidden geometry is invisible in its answers. \citet{schiekiera2026associations} report behavioural signal beyond cross-model consensus for word-similarity judgements; with our battery we find none.

\textbf{It holds on new kinds of context.} On the 400 contexts of the four new kinds, the answers beat the text for \GSixSThree\ models (\GSixAnsNew\ vs \GSixTextNew; preregistered; for Gemma and gpt-oss the interval includes 0).

\textbf{What crosses the API boundary.} Is the sharing a property of outputs in general, or of answers? We measured the own-model excess for each channel that an API can return, on 1{,}000 further fresh contexts, against the last layer (preregistered; \cref{fig:geometry}b): yes/no answers \GSevLAns; the next-token law reduced to its top 1, 5 and 20 tokens \GSevLTopOne, \GSevLTopFive\ and \GSevLTopTwenty; the full law under Fisher--Rao \GSevLFull; and the centred log-probability vectors \GSevLLogprob. With log-probability vectors, a model's outputs single out its own internals among the eight for \GSevBestLogprob\ models (agreement \GSevOwnLogprob, against \GSevOthersLogprob\ for the others); with answers, for \GSevBestAns. Text-level access reveals what models share; every returned token probability leaks a little of the model itself; the log-probability vectors, the linear image of the final state that \cref{thm:exact} inverts, reveal it (\GSevOut).

\textbf{A closed model.} Claude Haiku~5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of the samples give the same geometry: Spearman \GTwoSplit, above the bound of 0.7 fixed in advance from open models, and \GTwoText\ beyond the text geometry (preregistered). With the cross-fit estimator the geometry is close to Euclidean (negative eigenvalue mass \CFClaudeNegCF, against \CFClaudeNeg\ with the variance-subtraction rule). Most cells are unanimous, so this stability partly reflects how deterministic Claude is. Its agreement with the open models' hidden geometries is \GTwoOpen. With the same 24 questions and 8 samples, open models' answers agree with other open models' hidden geometries at 0.41--0.76 (median 0.58; exploratory), so the closed model reveals the shared geometry, at the lower end of the open models' range.

\textbf{The model-specific part needs log-probabilities.} The final hidden geometry of a model is its own, and \cref{thm:exact} recovers it exactly from full log-probabilities. On Qwen3-0.6B ($d=1{,}024$), from 1.15 million self-generated contexts, the recovered cosines of 2{,}000 held-out contexts correlate with the true ones at \GThreeBelow\ below $d(d+1)/2$ contexts, at \GThreeOneOne\ at $1.1\times$, and at $2\times$ the median error is $\GThreeErr$ (maximum $\GThreeMax$) (\cref{fig:geometry}c; preregistered). Closed APIs do not return full log-probabilities.

\textbf{Where answers lose.} For detecting \emph{that} a model's state moved (a hidden system prompt), sampled replies had more power than sampled answers at equal calls in every model and condition on development data, so we did not run that test (\cref{app:limits}).

\section{Application: monitoring closed agents}
\label{sec:results}
Because the observable geometry is shared, an open model that reads the same tokens holds a measured stand-in for a closed model's state (\cref{sec:geom-results}, S2). We use this to monitor closed agents: the monitor reads the transcript and the proposed action through an open model, which \cref{cor:action} says is enough for policy checks.
\textbf{Agents and monitors.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised; an environment checker labels each action. Open agents: Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B and Muse-Glimmer-30B. Closed agents, through their public APIs with reasoning off: Claude Haiku~5.5 and GPT-6-luna. The reader is Qwen3-8B (layer 32), with a logistic probe trained only on 17{,}623 Gemma-4 steps; baselines are the agent's own internals (where open), TF-IDF with the same labels, a zero-shot 70B judge and the action's tool alone.

\textbf{The transcript is enough.} \Cref{cor:action} says that the agent's internals cannot beat a monitor of the transcript and the action. A probe on Gemma-4's own internals scores 0.841 at the start of the turn and 0.979 after its reasoning; the reader probe, trained on the same labels, scores 0.994 ($+0.015$ [0.007, 0.026] over the internals) and is non-inferior on unseen task families (\cref{tab:main}). Given the action, adding the agent's hidden reasoning changes the AUROC by $-0.0014$ [$-0.0046$, $+0.0014$] (exploratory). The reader's state also replicates the agent's: on four architectures it reconstructs 46--66\% of the variance of the main components of the decision state on unseen scenarios, more than text features, and a probe trained on true states scores at least as well on the replica (\cref{app:agents,app:rb}). What the reader adds over word counts is robustness to shift: TF-IDF with the same labels ties it on the training benchmark but is at chance on AgentDojo (0.503 vs 0.781).

\textbf{Two closed agents.} Nothing was fit on the closed agents. At a cut set at the top 5\% of the agent's own unlabelled traffic, the monitor catches 94.1\% of Claude Haiku~5.5's 68 violations (zero-shot judge: 0\%; TF-IDF: 63.8\%) and 98.5\% of GPT-6-luna's (judge 37.9\%; TF-IDF 49.0\%). The scale of the scores does not transfer between agents (on Claude a cut at probability 0.5 catches 3\%), but the ranking does, so the cut must come from the monitored agent's traffic. Frozen after 500 actions, it keeps catching 94\% under bursts in which half the actions are violations, while a cut that moves over a window of 5{,}000 actions falls to 36\%, as \cref{prop:burst} predicts (\cref{fig:transfer}a).

\textbf{Live prevention.} We ran the monitor as a server in each closed agent's tool loop, on the same scenarios with and without the gate. In all four preregistered live tests the gate removed nearly all executed violations (\cref{tab:live,fig:prevention}), and no agent executed a violation after a block. Holding an escalated action for a reviewer kept the safety gain; its preregistered bound on the goal cost was not shown ($-0.020$ [$-0.051$, $+0.007$]; \cref{app:limits}). The round trip was \LATENCY.

\textbf{An agent that knows it is monitored, and an attacker who queries the score.} Told in its system prompt that a safety monitor reviews every tool call, Claude violated as often as before, and the monitor caught 97.1\% of its violations at the frozen cut (83.6\% when not told; $+0.136$ [0.050, 0.225]; preregistered). The notice moved all scores up, so the frozen cut escalated 15.2\% of actions instead of 5\%, within the Kolmogorov bound of \cref{prop:shift} ($K=0.24$); a cut set again on the told agent's unlabelled traffic escalated 4.7\% live and still removed every executed violation (\cref{tab:live}). An attacker who can query the score and rewrites only the free text of a violating call lowered the share caught from 96\% to 29\%; two preregistered defenses failed (51\% and 60\%), close to the ceiling of \cref{prop:ceiling} (\cref{app:limits}). The score must stay hidden from the agent.

\textbf{Labels of a new policy transfer from open agents.} Prompt injection on AgentDojo is a policy absent from our training data. Labels of it collected on two \emph{open} agents transfer to the \emph{closed} GPT-6-luna: AUROC 0.922 against the judge's 0.787 ($+0.136$ [0.078, 0.191]; \cref{tab:main}). The limit of a monitor is the coverage of its policies, not the agent.

\section{The tool}
\label{sec:tool}
We release a Python package and a website (links in the camera-ready version). One command, \texttt{graybox-geometry}, measures a chat model's observable geometry over the user's contexts: it reads $P(\textsc{yes})$ exactly where the API returns log-probabilities, and otherwise samples two independent halves and uses the cross-fit of \cref{thm:geometry}(d); it reports the number of calls and a cost estimate before it runs, and its tests check each theorem on cases with a known answer. For agents, a user imports logs (OpenAI or Anthropic format), labels actions and trains a monitor in the browser, where the logs never leave the machine; a guard places the monitor in a tool loop, an MCP proxy or one of three agent frameworks, and sets the cut again on new unlabelled traffic after a change of prompt (\cref{app:tool}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Information geometry and shared representations.} The Fisher metric and its uniqueness are classical \citep{cencov1982statistical,campbell1986extended,amari2016information}, as are the arcsine transform \citep{anscombe1948transformation} and classical scaling \citep{torgerson1952mds}; representational similarity analysis compares distance matrices without a fit \citep{kriegeskorte2008rsa}. Representations of different models converge \citep{huh2024platonic}. \citet{picozzi2026information} show that the Fisher--Rao geometry of next-token distributions is set by behaviour up to symmetries and shared across architectures; \citet{schiekiera2026associations} compare behavioural and hidden-state geometry in eight models. We measure the geometry through yes/no answers on sampled text, so it applies to closed models; we test it against hidden states with a cross-model control; and we use its sharing to justify monitoring closed agents through an open model.
\textbf{Black-box access.} Logits span a $d$-dimensional image \citep{finlayson2024logits}, and a production model's output projection is recovered up to an orthogonal map \citep{carlini2024stealing}; \cref{thm:exact} uses the same structure for the gauge-invariant hidden geometry. Replies serve to test whether an API serves a given model \citep{gao2025model}; in our data they are the more sensitive output for a change and the less faithful one for the state. Answers to questions serve as interpretable embeddings \citep{benara2024crafting}, detect lies with follow-up questions \citep{pacchiardi2024liar,cooney2026didyoulie,sam2025quere}, and forecast behaviour \citep{levy2026forecasting}; open readers probe closed transcripts \citep{conti2026hermeneutics}.
\textbf{Probes, monitors and control.} Probes on internals read truth, refusal and deception \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}, and CAPE probes an agent before each action \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. Chain-of-thought monitors catch reward hacking that action-only monitors miss \citep{baker2025monitoring,korbak2025cot,chen2025reasoning}; \cref{cor:action} delimits when the reasoning adds nothing. AI control audits a fixed budget of actions \citep{greenblatt2024control}; \cref{prop:frozen,prop:burst} give the budget a label-free, exact and burst-robust form. Latents made identifiable by training are recovered by complete encoders \citep{zhou2026identifiabilities}; \cref{prop:gauge} is the output-side counterpart.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
A closed model's hidden coordinates are not observable. Its answers reveal a geometry, exact in arcsine coordinates and estimable without bias from sampled text. That geometry tracks hidden geometry far better than the input text or the model's own replies, but it is the geometry that models share, not the model's own: a model's answers match other models' hidden states as well as its own, and an open model's state is the better stand-in. This makes monitoring closed agents through an open model a measured choice, and in our tests such a monitor predicts and prevents closed agents' violations live. The limits: agreement is \GOneAns\ on our first contexts and lower on fresh ones (\GSixAns), and much weaker within one kind of context; replies detect a change of state sooner; the model-specific geometry needs full log-probabilities, which closed APIs do not return; and an attacker who queries a monitor's score still evades it (\cref{app:limits}).

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work reads the geometry of a closed model's state from its answers and monitors closed AI agents before they act. The intended effect is safer deployment: in our tests, blocking flagged actions cut the episodes with an executed violation from about 7\% to 0.2\% on two closed agents. We see four risks. (1) A monitor can give false confidence: it catches violations of the policies that it was trained on and can miss a new policy until that policy has labels (\cref{app:limits}). (2) Blocking has a cost: goal completion fell by 1.8 points on GPT and 5.8 points on Claude. (3) An agent or attacker that can query the monitor's score can reword a violating call until it passes; the score should stay hidden from the agent. (4) Reading a model's state from outside could help an attacker to study a closed model. The geometry uses only ordinary answers; exact recovery needs full log-probabilities, which closed APIs do not return, and it recovers distances between states, not weights or private data. The code is released under the MIT license, and the browser tool does not upload the user's logs.

\section{The interrogation state in chat}
\label{app:psi}
On Claude Haiku~5.5, readouts of the interrogation state trained only on open models forecast which harmful requests Claude refuses. Against a three-level moderation verdict the forecast wins (three preregistered tests); against graded moderators it ties (prospective, 923 conversations: 0.873 vs 0.855). Combined with a moderator, it doubles the precision of an alarm for harmful requests that the model answers (0.535 vs 0.274).
