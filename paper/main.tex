\begin{abstract}
Safety tools that read a model's hidden state need its weights, and the agents with the most capability are closed.
We ask whether an outside observer can read the hidden state of a closed agent, replicate it, and use the replica to predict and prevent policy violations.
The raw coordinates of a hidden state are not identifiable from outputs: a rotation of the hidden space changes no output.
Two objects can be recovered, and we build a ``grey box'' from each.
The first is the behavioural state: the model's answers to fixed questions asked in side branches.
It recovers the hidden state of models with known internals (Othello-GPT: $R^2$ 0.967), but as a safety signal it only ties a text moderator.
The second follows from a simple theorem: an agent's internal state is a function of its tokens, so an open model that reads the same tokens can hold a replica of it.
From the transcript alone, a replica in an open reader's latent space reconstructs 66\% of the variance of the 128 main components of an agent's hidden state (51--54\% of each full layer), more than text features do (59\%). White-box readouts work on the replica (AUROC 0.987; true state 0.984) and also on a replica from text features (0.983): the information that decides a violation is in the tokens. The same reader replicates three more open agents with other architectures, on scenarios it never saw (46--54\% of the main components).
For a policy check on a proposed action, the transcript and the action are Bayes-sufficient.
A probe on the reader's latent state, trained only on an open agent, catches 94\% and 98\% of the violations of two closed agents, Claude Haiku~5.5 and GPT-6-luna (reasoning off), when the 5\% riskiest actions go to review, with a cut set without labels.
In the loop of two closed agents, it blocks violations before they run (episodes with an executed violation: GPT 6.6\% $\rightarrow$ 0.2\%; Claude 6.7\% $\rightarrow$ 0.2\%), at a cost in completed tasks (Claude: 40.7\% $\rightarrow$ 34.9\%). With a reviewer for the flagged actions, the safety gain stayed, but a small goal cost could not be excluded.
On an outside benchmark (AgentDojo), labels of a new policy collected on open agents fixed the monitor for a closed agent; on $\tau^2$-bench, the closed agents broke the policy too rarely to test this.
Every test is preregistered and hash-locked; we report the failures and the tests that could not be done.
We release an SDK and a website on which anyone can train and audit a monitor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}



Interpretability can read a model's hidden state: what it knows, and what it will do \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}.
For agents that act through tools, a probe on the hidden state can forecast an unauthorized action before it runs and stop it \citep{fayyazi2026cape}.
But these tools need the weights, and the agents with the most capability are closed.
Their providers give sampled text, and at most top-$k$ log probabilities and a summary of the reasoning.

We ask three questions.
\textbf{(1)} Can an outside observer read the hidden state of a closed model?
\textbf{(2)} Can it replicate the state well enough that white-box tools work on the replica?
\textbf{(3)} Can the replica predict and prevent the agent's violations, in real time?

\textbf{Answers.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{What can be read.} The raw coordinates of a hidden state are not identifiable from outputs (\cref{prop:gauge}). The behavioural state is, and a battery of side-branch questions recovers it on models with known internals (\cref{sec:psi}). As a safety signal this ``interrogation state'' is weak.
\item \emph{What can be replicated.} An agent's internal state is a function of the tokens that it read (\cref{prop:access}). An open reader model on the same tokens replicates 66\% of the agent's hidden-state variance, and readouts trained on the true state work on the replica (\cref{sec:replica}).
\item \emph{What it is good for.} For a policy check on a proposed action, the transcript and the action are Bayes-sufficient (\cref{cor:action}). A probe on the reader's latent state (the space that holds the replica), trained only on an open agent, catches most violations of two closed agents before they run, and blocks them live (\cref{sec:closed,sec:prevent}). Its limit is policy coverage (\cref{sec:coverage}).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Rigor.} Every confirmatory test was preregistered: its document and analysis code were hash-locked (SHA-256) before we ran it. The lock times are self-reported; for T13b, T16 and T20 the lock was also published before the result. \Cref{tab:all} lists the agent tests with their outcomes, including the ones that failed or could not be done.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\textbf{Setting.}
A monitor acts at step $t$, after the agent proposes an action and before the action runs.
$Z_t$ is every token that the agent read or wrote up to $t$; $V_t$ is the part that a deployment sees, and $H_t$ is the rest (e.g.\ hidden reasoning).
$A_t$ is the proposed action, and $W$ are world facts that the agent did not observe.
An internal representation is $\phi_t=\Phi(Z_t;\theta)$, a deterministic function of the tokens.

\begin{proposition}[gauge]\label{prop:gauge}
For any invertible $G$, the model whose hidden space is transformed by $G$ (with the adjacent weights compensated) computes the same function. Thus no method that uses only outputs can recover hidden coordinates, and a probe fit in one basis does not transfer to another. The behavioural state (the law of future outputs) is unchanged.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[access is not information]\label{prop:access}
For every label $Y$, $\sigma(Z_t,\phi_t)=\sigma(Z_t)$. Thus the best monitor that uses $(Z_t,\phi_t)$ is not better than the best monitor that uses $Z_t$, under every strictly proper score and at every point of the ROC curve. Under log loss, the best monitor that uses $Z_t$ beats the best that uses $V_t$ by exactly $I(Y;H_t\mid V_t)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[the proposed action is sufficient]\label{cor:action}
Let $Y=g(A_t,V_t,W)$ with $W\indep Z_t^-\mid V_t$, where $Z_t^-$ are the tokens before the action is sampled, and with sampling noise independent of $(Z_t^-,W)$. Then $P(Y\mid Z_t^-,A_t)=P(Y\mid V_t,A_t)$, and every monitor that reads internals at or before the action has a Bayes risk at least that of the best monitor that reads $(V_t,A_t)$.
\end{corollary}

Proofs are in \cref{app:proofs}.
\Cref{prop:gauge} says what a grey box can target: a behavioural state, or a state defined up to a learned map. It is the output-side counterpart of identifiability results for representations: latents that supervised training makes identifiable are recovered by encoders that are linear in them when the representation is complete \citep{zhou2026identifiabilities}, which is why we use a linear map between two models that read the same tokens.
\Cref{prop:access} says why a replica is possible: the hidden state is a function of tokens that an outside model can also read.
\Cref{cor:action} says when nothing is lost: a probe read before the action must also predict which action the agent samples; a monitor that reads the action does not.
It does not cover labels about later behaviour or about beliefs; for later behaviour, the rollout forecast dominates every readout of the state (\cref{app:proofs}).
What remains is statistical: learning $P(Y\mid V_t,A_t)$, which depends on the labels, the representation, and the coverage of the policies.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Agents and data.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched agent tasks in which one fact decides if an action is authorized; an environment rule checker labels each action. Open agents: Gemma-4 (26B) and gpt-oss-20b. Closed agents, through their public tool-use APIs, with reasoning off: Claude Haiku~5.5 and GPT-6-luna. External benchmark: AgentDojo prompt injections \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo}.

\textbf{The two grey boxes.} \emph{Interrogation state} $\Psi$: the probabilities of the answer ``yes'' to a fixed battery of questions, each asked in a side branch, so the real conversation never sees it. \emph{Reader replica}: Qwen3-8B reads the agent's instructions, the user's task, the earlier tool calls with their results, and the proposed action. A ridge map from its layer-32 residual stream gives the replica of the agent's state, and a linear probe on the same layer gives the monitor. The reader is trained only on Gemma-4 CAPE-Bench steps (17{,}623) and is never fit on a test agent.

\textbf{Baselines.} The agent's own internals (CAPE's probe); a zero-shot 70B judge (Llama-3.3-70B, same input); TF-IDF with the reader's input and labels; the action's tool alone.

\textbf{Statistics.} AUROC within strata (template, tool or suite) with 95\% intervals from a bootstrap over scenarios or episodes (1{,}000 resamples), and the share of violations caught when the 5\% highest-scored actions go to review, with a cut set without labels (the 95th percentile of the monitor's own scores on the agent's actions). The closed agents ran with reasoning off, so the transcript holds every token they produced.

\subsection{Reading the state: the interrogation state}
\label{sec:psi}
On models with known internals, $\Psi$ recovers the state.
A battery of 21 questions, designed with the states known (from a pool of 2{,}101), separates all 21 states of a program compiled into a transformer; a random battery of 256 questions separates 16 (development test, L0); after a random rotation of the residual stream, a readout that uses the known layout falls from $r=0.9995$ to $-0.46$, and $\Psi$ is unchanged (\cref{prop:gauge}).
On Othello-GPT, 61 queries recover the board-carrying hidden state at $R^2$ 0.967 on 1{,}000 fresh games (preregistered; input-only 0.873).
On eight open chat models, the answers recover the hidden state better than a text embedding ($+0.120$ $R^2$, 8/8 models, preregistered), and readouts trained only on open models transfer to Claude (harmful request 0.993, refusal 0.997 AUROC).
\textbf{As a safety signal, $\Psi$ is weak.} On agents, the answers alone do not match a white-box probe (T1). As a forecast of Claude's refusals, it only ties a graded text moderator in a prospective test (0.873 vs 0.855); with a moderator it doubles the precision of an alarm (\cref{app:psi}).

\subsection{Replicating the state: the reader replica (T15)}
\label{sec:replica}
On Gemma-4, where the true state is known, we fit a ridge map from the reader's latent space to the agent's hidden state at the decision point (5 layers, top 128 principal components, 80.5\% of the variance) on development steps.
On 12{,}128 fresh steps, the replica reconstructs 66.4\% of the variance from the transcript alone; text features reconstruct 58.6\% ($+0.079$ [0.075, 0.083]; \cref{fig:replica}).
A probe trained on the agent's \emph{true} states scores 0.984 on true fresh states and 0.987 on the replica ($+0.003$ [$-0.002$, $+0.007$]): white-box readouts work on the replica.
The replica is a conditional mean, so it is less spread than the true state, and a text replica also supports the readout (0.983): the part of the state that decides a violation is largely in the tokens, as \cref{prop:access} implies. A nonlinear map (an MLP) replicates less than the linear one (0.558 vs 0.664, exploratory), as expected when the reader represents the agent's main latents completely \citep{zhou2026identifiabilities}. Viewing attention as an associative memory over the tokens \citep{wang2026memory} predicts more interference in longer transcripts. Replication does fall with length (from 0.693 to 0.478 across quintiles of context length), but the probe on the replica stays at the level of the true state in every quintile (exploratory): the part of the state that decides a violation survives.

\textbf{Three more agents (T17).} One agent can be a special case. We repeated the test on three open agents with different architectures: a mixture of experts (gpt-oss-20b), hybrid linear attention (Qwen3.8-27B) and a dense model (Muse-Glimmer-30B). The split was stricter: development and fresh steps come from different scenarios. Before we read an agent's state, we checked that we give it the context that it saw. Teacher-forced, its own model predicts 91--100\% of the next 16 tokens of its actual tool call. The same reader (Qwen3-8B, layer 32) replicated each agent's decision state better than text features, and the probe trained on true states worked on every replica. All six preregistered endpoints pass (\cref{fig:replica_agents}). \REPLAGENTSTEXT{} Replication is partial: about half of the variance on unseen scenarios (Gemma-4 under the same split: \REPLGEMMASPLIT). The text map is at its best setting: its regularization chose the edge of the grid, and smaller values are worse for every agent (exploratory). The readout also works on the text replica, so again the part of the state that decides a violation is largely in the tokens.

\subsection{Why access does not help (T2--T5)}
\textbf{Timing.} A probe on the agent's internals scores 0.841 at the start of the turn and 0.979 after the reasoning (T2): the white-box advantage over turn-start monitors is when it reads.
\textbf{Labels.} Resampling scored by a 70B judge gets 0.782, below the probe (T3); the label function is the bottleneck.
With labels, a probe on the reader scores 0.994, above the agent's own internals (0.979; $+0.015$ [0.007, 0.026]; T4), and it is non-inferior on unseen task families (0.969 vs 0.976; T5).
Given the action, the agent's hidden reasoning adds nothing: $-0.0014$ [$-0.0046$, $+0.0014$] AUROC (exploratory), as \cref{cor:action} predicts.
\textbf{Representation.} TF-IDF with the same labels equals the reader on the training benchmark, but is worse on unseen families (0.950 vs 0.969) and at chance on AgentDojo (0.503 vs 0.781; T9).

\subsection{Two closed agent families (T8b, T11)}
\label{sec:closed}
Nothing was fit on the closed agents (\cref{tab:closed}).
On Claude, the monitor catches 94.1\% of 68 violations when the top 5\% of actions go to review (95.6\% with a cut set on labelled safe actions); the judge catches none.
On GPT-6-luna all three preregistered endpoints pass: pooled 0.996 vs 0.778 ($+0.217$ [0.174, 0.265]); against tool identity alone $+0.141$ [0.111, 0.171]; within tool $+0.039$ [0.015, 0.066].
Seen through Gemma's latent space (the T15 map and the true-state probe), Claude's actions score 0.959 and GPT's 0.976 (exploratory).
The probabilities do not transfer between agents (on Claude a 0.5 cut catches 3\%), but the ranking does: a threshold at the top 5\% of the agent's own unlabelled traffic, then frozen, catches 95.4\% online on Claude and 98.4\% on GPT, and stays at 94\% in a simulated burst of attacks, where a rolling threshold falls to 36\%.

\subsection{The limit is policy coverage (T10, T12--T14)}
\label{sec:coverage}
For a service-restart policy removed from training, the monitor still ranks Claude's violations well (0.912 vs 0.570), but escalates 0 of 26 at a 5\% budget; 20 labelled steps of the policy give 85\%, 40 give 94\% (T10, exploratory curve; \cref{fig:policy}a).
A held-out credentials policy with similar trained policies transfers with no labels (T12: the preregistered H1 fails, because there was nothing to fix).
Prompt injection on AgentDojo is a new policy: on GPT-6-luna, the monitor is not significantly better than the judge (0.840 vs 0.787; T13b fails).
We added labelled AgentDojo calls of the two \emph{open} agents and tested on the \emph{closed} agent (T14): 0.922 vs 0.787 ($+0.136$ [0.078, 0.191]). At the label-free cut, 58\% of the injected calls are caught, against 28\% without these labels (92\% vs 36\% with a cut set on benign calls; \cref{fig:policy}b). The open agents' calls came from the development user tasks, which the test shares; restricted to the confirmation split, whose user tasks the training never saw, the result holds (0.929 vs 0.786, $+0.143$ [0.075, 0.226]; label-free 54\% vs 29\%; exploratory).
On this benchmark, labels of a policy transferred from open agents to a closed agent. On $\tau^2$-bench we could not test this (T18; \cref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Live prevention on two closed agents (T16, T16b)}
\label{sec:prevent}
We ran the monitor as a server in the loop of GPT-6-luna on 500 CAPE-Bench scenarios, each with and without the gate (preregistered).
The threshold came from GPT's past, unlabelled traffic.
A blocked action does not run; the agent is told that it went to a human.
\PREVENTTEXT{}
\PREVENTBTEXT

\textbf{Hold for review (T16c).} Blocking has a cost: on Claude, goals fell by 5.8 points. In T16 and T16b, nearly all of that loss came from blocked \emph{safe} actions (96\% of it on Claude; exploratory). T16c sends an escalated action to a reviewer instead. The reviewer here is the environment's policy oracle, an upper bound on a human reviewer: it blocks a violation and lets a safe action run. \REVIEWTEXT

\section{The tool}
\label{sec:tool}
We release an SDK, a monitoring server and a website (links in the camera-ready version).
On the website, a user imports their agent's logs (OpenAI or Anthropic tool-calling format), labels actions, trains a monitor in the browser, and gets a cross-validated accuracy over held-out conversations, a frozen review threshold and the catch rate at their budget; the logs stay in the browser. A review queue ranks the unlabelled actions by risk: on 300 GPT-6-luna conversations with half of them labelled, its first 25 actions were all violations (base rate 4.7\%), and its first 43 held 92\% of the violations. A guard places the monitor in an OpenAI or Anthropic tool loop.
The browser and Python give byte-identical text on 302 real agent steps and the same held-out AUROC (0.9887 vs 0.9886).
The text monitor takes 0.18~ms per action; the reader takes 41~ms on one GPU, and the server round trip in the loop of a closed agent was \LATENCY.
A live viewer shows a closed chat model's interrogation state in a 3D space shared with open models, at about \$0.001 per turn.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Probes on internals} read truth, refusal and deception \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}; CAPE probes an agent before each action \citep{fayyazi2026cape}.
\textbf{Chain-of-thought monitors} catch reward hacking that action-only monitors miss \citep{baker2025monitoring,korbak2025cot,chen2025reasoning}; \cref{cor:action} says that for labels that the action and the visible facts decide, the reasoning adds nothing once the monitor sees the action.
\textbf{AI control} audits a fixed budget of actions with a trusted monitor \citep{greenblatt2024control}; we show that the threshold must come from the monitored agent's own traffic.
\textbf{Identifiability.} Hidden coordinates are identifiable only up to transformations that leave the function unchanged; supervised learning identifies latents linearly when the representation is complete and through sparsity otherwise \citep{zhou2026identifiabilities}. Our gauge result applies the same logic to an observer who sees only outputs.
\textbf{Black-box access} recovers parts of a model from logits \citep{carlini2024stealing,finlayson2024logits}, detects lies with follow-up questions \citep{pacchiardi2024liar,cooney2026didyoulie,sam2025quere}, forecasts behaviour from traces \citep{levy2026forecasting}, and probes closed transcripts with open readers \citep{conti2026hermeneutics}.
We add the identifiability and sufficiency theory, a direct test that the reader replicates the hidden state, closed agents from two providers, live prevention, and a preregistered test of each claim.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item We can test replication only where the true state is known (open agents). For closed agents, we test what the replica is for: prediction and prevention.
\item Most agent tests use one benchmark. On AgentDojo, a monitor without labels of the new policy is not significantly better than a judge on a closed agent (T13b). On $\tau^2$-bench \citep{barres2025tau2} (T18, 600 episodes), Claude Haiku~5.5 broke the airline policy in only 9 writes from 3 tasks, below the preregistered minimum of 15, so the test was not done. Descriptively, word counts trained on other agents' labels did better there than the reader (within-tool AUROC 0.963 vs 0.756).
\item Policy coverage was measured on three held-out policies; the role of similarity is a hypothesis.
\item \Cref{cor:action} needs a label that the action and the visible facts decide. It does not cover beliefs or later behaviour.
\item The interrogation state is weak as a safety signal; probes on the reader's latent state carry the safety results.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
The hidden state of a closed agent cannot be read in its own coordinates, but it can be partly replicated: an open model that reads the same tokens holds about half to two thirds of its main components, and white-box readouts work on the replica (as they do on text features, because the decisive information is in the tokens).
For the actions of closed agents with reasoning off, a probe on this grey box predicts and prevents violations on the benchmark it was trained on, at a cost in completed tasks. On a new benchmark it needs labels of the new policy; labels from open agents worked on AgentDojo.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work makes it possible to monitor closed AI agents before they act, from their transcripts alone. The intended effect is fewer harmful actions by deployed agents: in our tests, blocking the flagged actions cut the episodes with a violation from about 7\% to 0.2\% on two closed agents. We see three risks.
(1) A monitor can give false confidence. It catches violations of the policies that it was trained on, and it can miss a new policy until that policy has labels (\cref{sec:results}). We state this limit in the paper and in the tool.
(2) Blocking has a cost for users: goal completion fell by 1.8 points on GPT and 5.8 points on Claude.
(3) Methods that read the state of a model from outside could help an attacker to probe a closed model. Our methods use only the transcripts that the operator already has. They do not extract weights or private data.
We release the code under the MIT license. The tool runs in the user's browser and does not upload the user's logs.
