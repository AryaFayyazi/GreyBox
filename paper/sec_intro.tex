\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Interpretability reads what a model knows and what it will do from its hidden state \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}. For agents, a probe on the hidden state can stop an unsafe action before it runs \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. These tools need the weights. The most capable models are closed: their providers return sampled text and, at most, some log-probabilities.

\textbf{Why an outside read of the state.} Agents make calls that they are not authorized to make, and their words cannot be trusted to say so: in CAPE, four open frontier agents that knew a call was unauthorized made it and then denied it \citep{fayyazi2026cape}, and chain-of-thought often leaves out what drives an answer \citep{turpin2023unfaithful,chen2025reasoning}. A monitor needs the agent's state, read from outside its own account. For a closed agent the only outside access is its API.

\textbf{What can an API reveal of the hidden state?} Not hidden coordinates: any change of basis that the next layers undo leaves every output unchanged (\cref{prop:gauge}). Geometry survives. We ask the model yes/no questions in \emph{side branches} (\cref{fig:method}): copies of the conversation, each with one question appended, sent through its API and then discarded, so the agent never sees them. In the angles $\vartheta=2\arcsin\sqrt{P(\textsc{yes})}$, the Fisher--Rao distance between answer laws is Euclidean, its noise is known, and near a state it is the hidden geometry seen through the metric that the questions pull back (\cref{thm:geometry}). Nothing is trained.

\textbf{What we find} (\cref{fig:teaser}). The theory has three steps, and each makes a prediction that we test.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The geometry is estimable through the API} (\cref{sec:geom-results}). On eight open models, the answer geometry tracks the hidden geometry (0.41--0.74), better than embeddings of the input or replies on 6--7 of 8. Sampled answers reveal the part of the geometry that models share (a model's answers match another model's hidden geometry as well as its own; excess +0.002), while the model-private part appears only in log-probabilities. 8 policy questions agree with the linear slice of an agent's decision state that predicts them (0.36--0.60; full state 0.01--0.16; secondary).
\item \textbf{The geometry predicts violations} (\cref{sec:monitor,sec:forecast}). Where the transcript decides the violation (\cref{cor:action}), the estimated geometry suffices at the call: a fixed projection of it ranks violations nearly as well as a probe on the hidden state (0.82--0.99 on four open agents, 0.98 on GPT-6-luna), and the geometry alone, with no label and no question sign, flags them. Before the call, the forecast is the agent's policy (\cref{prop:selfforecast}), which its API supplies by sampling: the geometry of the agent's sampled next actions forecasts its violations with no label and no sign on Claude Haiku 5.5 (0.89, replicated at 0.87 on fresh episodes) and, on average, on four open agents across their task families (0.75; 3 of 4 above chance). Claude's words do not (GPT-6-luna's do): before 93\% of Claude's violating calls, none of its 32 answers about its next action admitted a violation (descriptive). With CAPE's checker labelling the sampled calls, the theory predicted in advance the loss of another model's draws on both closed agents (a first Claude test failed). A white-box probe forecasts better (by 0.16, registered); on Gemma-4 most of that lead comes from reading the agent's private reasoning, though our test was too small to show parity at the start of the turn.
\item \textbf{The geometry prevents violations} (\cref{sec:prevent-live}). On Claude's restarts, holding the calls its own read-out flags, with no human in the loop, cut violating episodes from 68\% to 35\%, against 43\% for random holds at the same dose; with CAPE's checker reviewing held calls, a law fixed in advance predicted five agents' reductions (an earlier form failed).
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Contributions.} Theory of what an API reveals and of monitoring versus forecasting (\cref{sec:method}; mostly elementary or classical, marked; its value is in predictions fixed before the data), evidence on eight open models, four open agents, two closed agents and three benchmarks, and a released tool (\cref{app:tool}).

\textbf{Preregistration.} We hash-locked every confirmatory test before its data existed (or, for a test on existing data, before its analysis) and published each lock before the run; a result is preregistered unless marked exploratory. Of the method's 80 tests, 36 passed every primary endpoint, 23 passed some, 13 failed, 2 stopped early and 6 are pending; \cref{app:prereg} lists every test with its outcome, and each claim below names the failed tests that bear on it. Analyses registered on existing data after related results were known are marked secondary; their positive Claude Haiku 5.5 results were retested on fresh episodes and replicated (their GPT-6-luna parts were not retested). Three headline tests redesign earlier tests that failed (the prevention law, the pre-call gate, Claude's forecast with exact contexts), reported next to them. Over 37 registered closed-agent endpoints through G64, 25 survive a Holm correction (exploratory); the later replications passed their own Holm families.
