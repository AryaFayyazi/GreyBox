\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Interpretability reads what a model knows and what it will do from its hidden state \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}. For agents, a probe on the hidden state can stop an unsafe action before it runs \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. These tools need the weights. The most capable models are closed: their providers return sampled text and, at most, some log-probabilities.

\textbf{Why ask the agent, not only read its text.} Agents make calls that they are not authorized to make, and what they say about it can mislead: in CAPE, four open frontier agents that knew a call was unauthorized made it and then denied it \citep{fayyazi2026cape}, and chain-of-thought often leaves out what drives an answer \citep{turpin2023unfaithful,chen2025reasoning}. We find the same gap in a closed agent: before its call, Claude Haiku 5.5's answers about its next action do not forecast its coming violation, nor does the same model reasoning over the full transcript, but its own next actions, sampled through its API, do. An agent's next action follows from its state, which the text does not show. Through the API, the agent's policy can still be sampled: so we read what the agent does, not only what it says.

\textbf{What can an API reveal?} Not hidden coordinates: any change of basis that the next layers undo leaves every output unchanged (\cref{prop:gauge}). Geometry survives. We ask the model yes/no questions in \emph{side branches} (\cref{fig:method}): copies of the conversation, each with one question appended, sent through its API and then discarded, so the agent never sees them. In the angles $\vartheta=2\arcsin\sqrt{P(\textsc{yes})}$, the Fisher--Rao distance between answer laws is Euclidean (\cref{thm:geometry}), and its noise is known. Nothing is trained.

\textbf{What we find} (\cref{fig:teaser}).
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{An API reveals a shared geometry} (\cref{sec:geom-results}). On held-out contexts of eight open models, the answer geometry tracks each model's hidden geometry better than sentence embeddings of the input or the replies (on 6 or 7 of 8 models against the strongest of five), and it is shared across models (own-model excess +0.002).
\item \textbf{At the call, any capable reader suffices} (\cref{sec:monitor,sec:carry}). The transcript and the call decide a violation (\cref{cor:action}). A fixed read-out of the agent's answers, never fit to labels, ranks violations nearly as well as a probe on hidden states (0.82--0.99 on four open agents, 0.98 on GPT-6-luna), and another model's answers do as well.
\item \textbf{Before the call, the forecast is the agent's policy} (\cref{prop:selfforecast,sec:forecast}), which the API supplies by sampling; from checked samples, the theory predicts how much a forecaster that does not sample loses. On Claude Haiku 5.5's one task family with varying violations, among calls at the same step, its self-report (0.47) and two reasoning text monitors (at most 0.60) are near chance or weak, while its own sampled next actions forecast which call violates (0.89). Another model given the same context does not (0.54). These results replicated on 492 fresh episodes. GPT-6-luna's words, by contrast, track its actions (self-report 0.84), a reading we made after the data.
\item \textbf{The agent can police itself} (\cref{sec:prevent-live}). With no human in the loop, holding a call that the agent's own read-out flags cut Claude Haiku 5.5's violating episodes from 68\% to 35\%, against 43\% for random holds at the same dose; a plain policy reminder did better still (18\%).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Contributions.} Theory of what an API reveals and of monitoring versus forecasting (\cref{sec:method}; mostly elementary or classical, marked; its value is in predictions fixed before the data), evidence on eight open models, four open agents, two closed agents and three benchmarks, and a released tool (\cref{app:tool}).

\textbf{Preregistration.} We hash-locked every confirmatory test before its data existed (or, for a test on existing data, before its analysis), and published the later locks before the run. A result is preregistered unless marked exploratory. Of the method's 74 tests, 34 passed every primary endpoint, 22 passed some, 13 failed, and the rest stopped early or are pending; \cref{app:prereg} lists every test with its outcome, and each claim below names the failed tests that bear on it. Some analyses (the geometric monitor and forecast, the loss prediction, the lift across steps) used data that already existed; we locked each before its analysis, but registered them after related results were known, so we mark them as secondary (\cref{app:prereg}). For Claude Haiku 5.5 we then replicated each of their positive results on fresh episodes, in tests registered before those episodes existed, and every one replicated; their GPT-6-luna parts were not replicated. Some headline tests redesign earlier tests that failed (the prevention law, the pre-call gate, and Claude's forecast with exact contexts); the failed versions are reported next to them. Over all 37 registered endpoints of the closed-agent tests behind the abstract, passed or failed, 26 passed as registered and 25 survive a Holm correction (exploratory; \cref{app:prereg}).
