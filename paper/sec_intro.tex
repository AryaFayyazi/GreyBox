\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Interpretability reads what a model knows and what it will do from its hidden state \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}. For agents, a probe on the hidden state can stop an unsafe action before it runs \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. These tools need the weights. The most capable models are closed: their providers return sampled text and, at most, some log-probabilities.

\textbf{Why an outside read of the state.} Agents make calls that they are not authorized to make, and their words cannot be trusted to say so: in CAPE, four open frontier agents that knew a call was unauthorized made it and then denied it \citep{fayyazi2026cape}, and chain-of-thought often leaves out what drives an answer \citep{turpin2023unfaithful,chen2025reasoning}. A monitor needs the agent's state, read from outside its own account. For a closed agent the only outside access is its API.

\textbf{What can an API reveal of the hidden state?} Not hidden coordinates: any change of basis that the next layers undo leaves every output unchanged (\cref{prop:gauge}). Geometry survives. We ask the model yes/no questions in \emph{side branches} (\cref{fig:method}): copies of the conversation with one question appended, discarded after the answer, so the agent never sees them. In the angles $\vartheta=2\arcsin\sqrt{P(\textsc{yes})}$, the Fisher--Rao distance between answer laws is Euclidean, its noise is known, and near a state it is the hidden geometry seen through the metric that the questions pull back (\cref{thm:geometry}). Nothing is trained.

\textbf{What we find} (\cref{fig:teaser}). The theory has three steps, and each makes a prediction that we test.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The geometry is estimable through the API} (\cref{sec:geom-results}). On eight open models, the answer geometry tracks the hidden geometry (0.41--0.74), better than embeddings of the input or replies on 6--7 of 8. Sampled answers reveal the part of the geometry that models share (own-model excess +0.002), while the model-private part appears only in log-probabilities. 8 policy answers are linearly predictable from an agent's decision state across scenarios (0.36--0.60), though their geometry agrees with the full state at only 0.01--0.16 (secondary).
\item \textbf{The geometry predicts violations} (\cref{sec:monitor,sec:forecast}). Where the transcript decides the violation (\cref{cor:action}), the estimated geometry suffices at the call: a fixed projection of it ranks violations nearly as well as a probe on the hidden state (0.82--0.99 on four open agents, 0.98 on GPT-6-luna), and the geometry alone, with no label and no question sign, flags them. Before the call, the forecast is the agent's policy (\cref{prop:selfforecast}), which its API supplies by sampling: the geometry of the agent's sampled next actions forecasts its violations with no label and no sign on Claude Haiku 5.5 (0.89, replicated at 0.87 on fresh episodes) and, on average, on four open agents across their task families (0.75; 3 of 4 above chance). Claude's words do not (GPT-6-luna's do): before 93\% of Claude's violating calls, none of its 32 answers about its next action admitted a violation (descriptive). The theory predicted in advance a forecaster's loss on both closed agents (a first Claude test failed). A white-box probe forecasts better (by 0.16 on two agents, registered); on Gemma-4 much of that lead may come from reading the agent's private reasoning, but our test was too small to show parity at the start of the turn.
\item \textbf{The geometry prevents violations} (\cref{sec:prevent-live}). On Claude's restarts, with no human in the loop, a gate on the estimated geometry alone left 20\% of episodes violating, against 32\% for random holds at the same dose (a first version that broke ties at random did not beat them); the signed read-out also beat random holds (35\% vs 43\%), and holds stack on a policy reminder (21\% to 11\%); with CAPE's checker reviewing held calls, a law fixed in advance predicted five agents' reductions (an earlier form failed).
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Why the closed-agent results bear on the geometry.} On open agents, the same statistic tracks the true hidden geometry and forecasts violations; on Claude, whose states we cannot see, its geometry matches the open models' and it forecasts and gates: converging evidence, not proof, that the slice carries over.

\textbf{Contributions.} The first training-free estimate of hidden-state geometry from a closed API's sampled answers, with exactly known noise, checked against true hidden states (prior work needs log-probabilities or weights, or never makes this check; its parts are classical, \cref{sec:related}); its use to forecast and prevent violations of closed agents; evidence on eight open models, six agents and three benchmarks; a released tool (\cref{app:tool}).

\textbf{Preregistration.} We hash-locked every confirmatory test before its data existed (or, for a test on existing data, before its analysis) and published each lock before the run; a result is preregistered unless marked exploratory. Of the method's 82 tests, 38 passed every primary endpoint, 26 passed some, 14 failed, 2 stopped early and 2 are pending; \cref{app:prereg} lists every test with its outcome, and each claim below names the failed tests that bear on it. Analyses registered after related results were known are marked secondary; Claude's positive ones replicated on fresh episodes (GPT-6-luna's were not retested). Three headline tests redesign earlier tests that failed (the prevention law, the pre-call gate, Claude's forecast with exact contexts), reported next to them. Of 37 closed-agent endpoints, 25 survive Holm (exploratory; \cref{app:prereg}).
