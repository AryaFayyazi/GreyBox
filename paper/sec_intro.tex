\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Interpretability reads what a model knows and what it will do from its hidden state \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,zou2023repe,arditi2024refusal,goldowskydill2025probes}. For agents, a probe on the hidden state can stop an unsafe action before it runs \citep{fayyazi2026cape}. These tools need the weights. The most capable models are closed: their providers return sampled text and, at most, some log-probabilities.

\textbf{Why ask the agent, not only read its text.} Agents make calls that they are not authorized to make, and what they say about it can mislead: in CAPE, four open frontier agents that knew a call was unauthorized made it and then denied it \citep{fayyazi2026cape}, and chain-of-thought often leaves out what drives an answer \citep{turpin2023unfaithful,chen2025reasoning}. We find the same gap in a closed agent, on unapproved production restarts, the one CAPE-Bench task family where its violations vary. Before its call, on the same calls and among calls at the same step, Claude Haiku 5.5's answers about its next action do not forecast its coming violation (0.47), nor does the same model reasoning over the full transcript (0.45), but its own next actions, sampled through its API, do (0.89). An agent's next action follows from its state, which the text does not show. Through the API, the agent's policy can still be sampled: so we read what the agent does, not only what it says.

\textbf{What can an API reveal?} Not hidden coordinates: any change of basis that the next layers undo leaves every output unchanged (\cref{prop:gauge}). Geometry survives. We ask the model yes/no questions in \emph{side branches} (\cref{fig:method}): copies of the conversation, each with one question appended, sent through its API and then discarded, so the agent never sees them. In the angles $\vartheta=2\arcsin\sqrt{P(\textsc{yes})}$, the Fisher--Rao distance between answer laws is Euclidean (\cref{thm:geometry}), and its noise is known. Nothing is trained.

\textbf{What we find} (\cref{fig:teaser}). \emph{(i) What an API reveals} (\cref{sec:geom-results}): on held-out contexts of eight open models, the answer geometry tracks each model's hidden geometry better than the input or the replies do, and it is shared across models (own-model excess +0.002); Claude Haiku 5.5's answers agree with open models' hidden geometry at 0.45--0.51. \emph{(ii) At the call, the geometry flags violations, and any capable reader suffices} (\cref{sec:monitor,sec:carry}): a call's Fisher--Rao distance from the agent's typical state, with no label and no question sign, ranks the violating calls among both closed agents' sampled calls (0.82; GPT-6-luna's interval is wide); the transcript and the call decide a violation (\cref{cor:action}), so a fixed read-out of the agent's answers, never fit to labels, ranks violations nearly as well as a probe on its hidden states, carries to two external benchmarks and two closed agents, and another model's answers do as well or better. \emph{(iii) Before the call, the forecast is the agent's policy} (\cref{prop:selfforecast,sec:forecast}), and text suffices only when the agent's words track its actions. The theory predicts, with no label, how much a text monitor loses against the policy; for three text monitors on both closed agents, the prediction matched the observed loss to within 0.01. For GPT-6-luna they do: its self-report and a reasoning text monitor forecast its coming violation. For Claude Haiku 5.5 they do not: both are at or below chance, and GPT-6-luna reasoning over Claude's transcript does little better, while Claude's own sampled next actions forecast the violation (\cref{cor:rollout}), as does their distance from its typical state in the geometry. GPT-6-luna, reading Claude's sampled actions, forecasts better still: what text lacks is the agent's policy, which the agent's API supplies. A label-free check tells the two cases apart (exploratory): the rank agreement between what an agent says about its next action and what its sampled policy does ($-0.01$ for Claude Haiku 5.5, $+0.25$ for GPT-6-luna). \emph{(iv) Prevention} (\cref{sec:prevent-live}): with no human in the loop, the agents' own read-outs, holding a call once for it to be re-checked, cut violating episodes from 67\% to 35\% (Claude Haiku 5.5) and from 27\% to 17.5\% (GPT-6-luna), more per hold than random holds.

\textbf{Contributions.} Theory of what an API reveals and of monitoring versus forecasting (\cref{sec:method}; mostly elementary or classical, marked; its value is in predictions fixed before the data), evidence on eight open models, four open agents, two closed agents and three benchmarks, and a released tool (\cref{app:tool}).

\textbf{Preregistration.} We hash-locked every confirmatory test before its data existed (or, for a test on existing data, before its analysis), and published the later locks before the run. A result is preregistered unless marked exploratory. Of the method's tests, many passed and some failed; \cref{app:prereg} lists every test with its outcome, and each claim below names the failed tests that bear on it. Some headline tests redesign earlier tests that failed (the prevention law, the pre-call gate, and Claude's forecast with exact contexts); the failed versions are reported next to them. Over all 26 registered endpoints of the closed-agent tests that the abstract cites, 20 passed as registered and 17 survive a Holm correction (exploratory; \cref{app:prereg}).
