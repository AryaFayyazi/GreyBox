\section{Estimating the hidden geometry through the API}
\label{sec:geom-results}
We first test the reconstruction where the truth is visible: on open models, whose hidden states we store.
\textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (1{,}440 held out) and 1{,}000 fresh ones with four new kinds; 88 questions; eight open models from seven families (\cref{app:details}). Ground truth: each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at a layer chosen on development data (\cref{app:details}).

\textbf{Answers beat the input and the replies.} On every model, the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input does (gains \GOneGain; every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; \cref{fig:geometry}a, \cref{tab:geometry}). The model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, do little better than the input (\GFiveRep). Agreement rises with more questions (\GOneMore); 8 sampled answers per question keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts (400 of them of four new kinds), answers beat the input on 6 of 8 models. Against the strongest of five text baselines (three sentence embedders, on the input or the replies), answers win on 7 of 8 models on held-out contexts (gpt-oss ties) and on 6 of 8 on fresh ones (Gemma-3 and gpt-oss lose by 0.10 and 0.15; exploratory). Raw $P(\textsc{yes})$ ranks about as well; the angles add a known noise (\cref{sec:monitor}).

\textbf{The geometry is shared across models.} A model's answers match another model's hidden geometry exactly as well as its own. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the mean own-model excess is \GSixExcess, inside the equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ that we fixed in advance (\cref{fig:geometry}b). So what the answers reveal is the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}. It also predicts the next section's result at the call: the shared part is what any model's answers can read.

\textbf{Which channel reveals what.} On 1{,}000 further fresh contexts (preregistered), sampled yes/no answers carry no own-model excess (+0.001; $-$0.006 at the last layer), while at the last layer the model-private part grows with the output channel (top-1 +0.056, top-20 +0.115, log-probabilities +0.221 [0.211, 0.230]); log-probabilities pick out the model in 7 of 8 cases, answers in 1. Yes/no answers through a closed API therefore expose the shared geometry of the state (free text can still identify a model \citep{pasquini2024llmmap,gao2024model}), and the private part only through actions.

\textbf{A short battery estimates a slice of the decision state.} By \cref{thm:geometry}(c), $m$ questions estimate the hidden geometry in the metric they pull back, a slice of rank at most $m$, not the full state. At agents' proposed calls, 8 policy questions agree with the full decision-state geometry at only 0.01--0.16; a linear map from the decision state to the answers, fit across scenarios with no label, gives the slice they pull back, and the answer geometry agrees with those predictions at 0.60, 0.36, 0.38 and 0.58 on the four open agents, against 0.02--0.10 for random rank-8 maps of the state (all 4 lower bounds above both; secondary). 32 broader questions recover more of the full state (+0.12 [0.08, 0.16], still 0.18) but do not flag violations (0.50; policy battery 0.58--0.96).

\textbf{A closed model reveals the shared geometry, as open models' answers do.} On 192 held-out contexts, two halves of Claude Haiku 5.5's answers to 24 questions give the same geometry (Spearman \GTwoSplit), which agrees with open models' hidden geometries at \GTwoOpen. On fresh contexts of new kinds (news, code, jailbreaks, injections), these 24 answers failed a registered test (0.10--0.48; its embedded replies did better), because the battery was small: open models given the same 24 questions score 0.10--0.38 there (exploratory). With all 88, on 200 further fresh contexts (registered), Claude agreed with the open models' hidden geometries at 0.26--0.64 and beat the strongest of five text baselines, its replies included, on 7 of 8 models (mean +0.21 [0.15, 0.27]). By kind (exploratory): +0.36 on the six original kinds, +0.02 [$-$0.06, 0.10] on new kinds, where open models' own exact answers do little better (+0.08); the battery limits new kinds. With no own-model excess, triangulation (\cref{prop:triang}) estimates Claude's agreement with its own hidden geometry at 0.49 [0.44, 0.55] (leave-one-out error \GVtMae; 0.114 on fresh contexts).

\section{Monitoring, forecasting and preventing violations}
\label{sec:results}
\textbf{Agents and benchmarks.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised; its checker labels each action. Open agents (decision states stored): Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B, Muse-Glimmer-30B. Closed agents, through their APIs, reasoning off: Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6-luna. External benchmarks: AgentDojo and Sierra's $\tau^2$-bench airline.

\subsection{Monitoring at the call}
\label{sec:monitor}
\textbf{The read-out.} At each proposed call, the agent answers 8 yes/no questions about it through its own API, 4 times each (\cref{app:questions}), scored by the fixed read-out (\cref{eq:readout}).

\textbf{It ranks violations about as well as a probe on the hidden states.} On held-out calls, the within-template AUROC is \GFtFixed\ (\cref{tab:g15}). A probe trained on true decision states (it needs weights and labels) scores \GFtTrainedWB; the mean difference is \GFtTtwo. The read-out beats the agent's single self-audit answer by \GFtTone. On the open agents, it also beats a Gemma-4 judge that reasons, by \GTwJone. On Claude Haiku 5.5, whose answers we take with reasoning off, the judge is better (\cref{tab:g15}). The read-out reads the slice of the state its questions pull back (\cref{sec:geom-results}), not the full state: on open agents about 20\% [13, 27] of its ranking power runs through the hidden violation direction, the rest through the transcript, which any monitor can see.

\textbf{With no label, the read-out's SNR predicts where it works.} With the known noise (\cref{thm:geometry}), the read-out's variance beyond it, its SNR, needs no label (\cref{prop:snrceil}); over 29 cells the SNR predicts the AUROC (Spearman 0.71; raw variance 0.67): cells with SNR $>0$ score 0.96, the rest 0.68.

\textbf{At the call, another answerer does as well, as the theory implies.} The transcript decides the violation (\cref{cor:action}) and the answers read a shared geometry, so another model answering about the call should match the agent: on open agents it does (own $-$ other +0.012 [$-$0.018, 0.040]); on Claude it does better ($-$0.09 on restarts).

\textbf{Other benchmarks and closed agents.}\label{sec:carry} On AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, 8 questions written from the injection policy before any answer existed rank the open agents' injected calls at \GSvGemma\ and \GSvGpt. GPT-6-luna's answers rank its violations at \GGlAt. Claude Haiku 5.5's rank its unapproved restarts at \GFtClosedAFixed, but pooled score 0.475: a fact it never checked decides its mailing-list sends; on $\tau^2$-bench, \GEeRone.

\subsection{Forecasting before the call}
\label{sec:forecast}
\input{tab_forecast}
\textbf{The pre-call state.} Before each call, the agent answers 8 questions about its next action (its self-report); \cref{tab:forecast} compares every forecaster on the same calls.

\textbf{When text suffices: GPT-6-luna.} Its words track its actions: its self-report (0.84) and a text monitor (0.78) forecast as well as its sampled actions (0.73), and so does Gemma-4 sampling from its contexts (0.78; descriptive); read after the data, as the registered opposite expectation failed.

\textbf{When text does not suffice: Claude Haiku 5.5.} Claude Haiku 5.5 rarely violates (2 of 24 CAPE-Bench templates; 1.5--1.6\% on AgentDojo and $\tau^2$), and on one template it violates nearly always; its tests use the other, unapproved production restarts (41 scenarios), with exact contexts.

Among calls at the same step, its self-report and both reasoning text monitors are near chance or weak; only its sampled next actions forecast which call violates (\cref{tab:forecast}; own $-$ self-report \GSeDiff; own $-$ GPT-6-luna's judge \GXjDiff). Its words are nearly constant: before every violating call, each answer to whether it would comply said yes, and before 93\% (94\% fresh) of them none of its 32 answers admitted a violation; its rare admissions came before safe calls (descriptive). Gemma-4 sampling from Claude's contexts does not (0.54 at the same step).

Across steps, Gemma-4's draws tie Claude's own (0.82 vs 0.80; exploratory; the registered test on rebuilt contexts found Gemma-4's draws better, $-$0.14 [$-$0.20, $-$0.09]), and Claude's draws alone (0.80) score below the step alone (0.82): across steps, other readers track \emph{when} Claude violates; within a step, only its own policy tells \emph{which} call (+0.35 [0.28, 0.42]; exploratory). The sampled actions still add to the best text monitor across steps (0.90 to 0.96; on GPT-6-luna they do not, 0.78 to 0.73, descriptive). 

\textbf{The registered results replicated.} On 492 fresh episodes (\GRpN\ calls), in a test registered with its Holm correction before any episode existed, all 8 endpoints passed: own draws $-$ self-report \GRpOne\ (primary), $-$ the two text monitors \GRpTwo\ and \GRpThree, lift across steps \GRpFour\ (the lift was first found on existing data, secondary). The policy read costs \$0.002 per decision (median 9\,s). On $\tau^2$-bench the forecast was not above chance (0.61 [0.44, 0.84]). Sampling, not the reader, matters: GPT-6-luna reading Claude's sampled calls beats reading the transcript (\GOrDone; fresh \GRpSeven) and Claude's own reads.

\textbf{The geometry alone forecasts.} With no label and no question sign, the mean distance of the agent's sampled next actions from its typical state in the estimated geometry forecasts its coming violation: 0.89 on Claude Haiku 5.5 (0.87 on fresh episodes, registered before they existed), weakly 0.64 on GPT-6-luna, and on average 0.75 [0.68, 0.81] over four open agents across their task families (lowest Muse-Glimmer 0.56 [0.44, 0.68]; secondary: on existing draws). The signal comes from sampling (exploratory): on Claude's one template, a rule that knows the risky tool (the share of draws that restart) does better (0.95; fresh 0.93), and so does CAPE's checker on the draws (0.95); across the open agents' task families, where no single tool is risky, a task-agnostic rule (the share of draws that write) is weak: on 1{,}189 decisions no earlier draw used (registered), the geometry beat it on all four agents (0.77 vs 0.40; +0.37 [0.30, 0.44]), and the checker did better again (0.85). On 260 new restart worlds from CAPE's generator (registered), both forecasts scored 0.90 [0.86, 0.94]; within this family a generic rule reading the samples as writes did better (0.93; failed: $-$0.03).

\textbf{What the weights add.} A probe on CAPE's stored decision state, read after the agent's hidden reasoning, forecasts better (registered comparison failed: 0.16 [0.04, 0.28]; Gemma-4 0.97 vs 0.78, gpt-oss-20b 0.82 vs 0.70). At the turn's start, where a closed API forecasts from, a registered test on 865 fresh Gemma-4 units set a frozen white-box probe (0.83) against the API: the signed API forecast was non-inferior (0.83; +0.004 [$-$0.045, 0.053], bar $-$0.05); the label-free geometry, the primary, fell just short (0.81; $-$0.026 [$-$0.076, 0.026]). The lead comes from the private reasoning (turn-start minus after-reasoning probe $-$0.14 [$-$0.18, $-$0.10]), which no monitor of a closed agent sees.

\textbf{The reconstructed geometry alone flags violations.} Before each step, both closed agents drew calls in side branches, and CAPE's checker labelled them. With no label and no question sign, a drawn call's Fisher--Rao distance from the agent's typical state ranks the violating ones: \GGeoClaude\ on Claude Haiku 5.5 (1{,}765 calls, restarts only, so within tool) and \GGeoGpt\ on GPT-6-luna (832 calls; the interval is wide; secondary). The signed read-out of \cref{sec:monitor} is one projection of this geometry; it adds the direction of each question and scores higher (0.89 and 0.99 on the same calls). On the fresh episodes, the geometric monitor scored 0.81.

\textbf{The theory predicts the loss of any forecaster that does not sample.} By \cref{prop:selfforecast}, a forecast $q$ from the context alone loses $\E(f-q)^2$ in Brier score against the agent's policy $f$; the agent's draws, checked by CAPE's checker, estimate this without a label on the executed call (\cref{cor:rollout}). Registered in advance, the predicted loss of another model's draws matched the observed on both closed agents: 0.122 vs 0.126 on Claude (+0.004 [$-$0.003, 0.010]; a first test on rebuilt contexts failed) and 0.018 vs 0.020 on GPT-6-luna (bar $\pm0.02$). On the fresh episodes they matched again (\GRpSixA\ and \GRpSixB); so did three text monitors' (secondary). The prediction needs checked draws: with GPT-6-luna's judgments in place of the checker it failed, even after recalibration. A registered Brier separation of the two regimes failed (poor calibration).

\subsection{Preventing violations}
\label{sec:prevent-live}
\textbf{Self-policing.} A side branch asks the agent whether a non-read call complies with its policy; a flagged call goes to review, preventing the flagged share (\cref{prop:prevention}). With CAPE's checker as reviewer (\cref{tab:selfpolice}), Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes, Claude Haiku 5.5 69\% of those its transcript decides (31\% over all), and AgentDojo attack success fell by 84\% and 50\%. Offline flag rates predicted five agents' live reductions within 0.15 (mean error 0.035; \cref{fig:law}; an earlier form failed).

\textbf{A gate on the pre-call state.} At an equal review rate on fresh GPT-6-luna episodes, a gate on the pre-call read-out cut violating episodes from 31.7\% to 7.9\%, one on the step to 17.9\% (\GLbPmP; an earlier design failed; on Gemma-4 it did not prevent).

\textbf{Prevention with no reviewer.} A flagged call is held once and the agent told to re-check; an identical re-proposal runs. Holding the calls Claude's signed read-out flags cut violating episodes from 67.9\% to 35.2\%, against 42.9\% for random holds at a matched dose (relative reductions; +0.114 [0.035, 0.200]; not Holm in the full family). The same signed gate on GPT-6-luna (six templates) cut them from \GSgGNone\ to \GSgGSelf, against \GSgGRand\ for random holds (\GSgGDiff, not Holm, mostly one template; success \GSgGSucc, failed narrowly); nothing changed where an unchecked fact decides it. 

\textbf{The geometry alone gates, beyond random holds.} A gate on the distance from the agent's typical state (cut set on unlabelled calls; no label, no question sign) first failed to beat random holds at a matched dose on Claude (44.9\% vs 48.2\% violating), because Claude's answers tie and half of the violating calls sat at the cut, where it held by a coin (exploratory). Registered with ties held, the gate left 20.1\% of episodes violating against 32.3\% for random holds at the same dose (0.32 vs 0.30 holds per call; +0.122 [0.071, 0.177]). On GPT-6-luna, across six task families on 360 fresh worlds, the same gate left 9.7\% of episodes violating against 15.3\% for random holds at its dose (+0.056 [0.019, 0.092]; success $-$0.04 [$-$0.09, 0.01]; the random arm ran after the first comparison, which held half as many random calls). On 300 new restart worlds the frozen gate failed (22.3\% vs 25.0\%): it held 84\% of violating calls, as before, but Claude, held once, violated anyway in 39\% of those episodes (26\% before; exploratory). Registered next on fresh episodes of 130 of those worlds, holding again while the re-read distance stayed at or above the cut (up to 3 holds) left 7.7\% violating against 26.2\% for random holds at the same dose (+0.185 [0.100, 0.269]; success +0.02). Forecast-timed reminders did no better than random (\GFsFc\ vs \GFsRand).
