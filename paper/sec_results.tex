\section{Reconstructing the hidden geometry from answers}
\label{sec:geom-results}
We first test the reconstruction where the truth is visible: on open models, whose hidden states we store.
\textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (3{,}360 development, 1{,}440 confirmation). Two more sets of 1{,}000 fresh contexts add four new kinds: news, programming, jailbreaks and prompt injections. We ask 88 questions about the message, on eight open chat models from six families. The ground truth is each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at a layer chosen on development data. All results are on held-out contexts (\cref{app:details}).

\textbf{Answers beat the input and the replies.} On every model, the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input does (gains \GOneGain; every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; \cref{tab:geometry}). The model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, do little better than the input (\GFiveRep). Agreement rises with the number of questions (from 8 to 88: \GOneMore), and 8 sampled answers per question keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts of four new kinds, answers beat the input on 6 of 8 models.

\textbf{The geometry is shared across models.} A model's answers match another model's hidden geometry exactly as well as its own. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the mean own-model excess is \GSixExcess, inside the equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ that we fixed in advance (\cref{fig:geometry}b); the hidden geometries of different models also agree with each other (\GSixHH). So what the answers reveal is the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}. This finding drives the rest of the paper: the shared part is what any model's answers can read.

\textbf{A closed model reveals the same shared geometry.} Claude Haiku 5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of its answers give the same geometry (Spearman \GTwoSplit; bound fixed in advance: 0.7), which carries \GTwoText\ beyond the input embedding. Its answers agree with open models' hidden geometries at \GTwoOpen.

\textbf{Its own hidden geometry, by triangulation.} Its hidden states are not visible. If, as on open models, no part of its answers is specific to its own state, its own agreement equals its loading on the shared geometry times its hidden states' loading (\cref{prop:triang}). Leaving each open model out, as if closed, this estimate predicts its true agreement to within \GVtMae\ on average (\GVtInside\ of 8 inside the predicted interval; largest miss 0.22, on gpt-oss-20b). For Claude Haiku 5.5 it gives 0.49, with interval [0.32, 0.53] over the range of open models' loadings. On the fresh contexts the same check was weaker (mean error 0.11; 4 of 8 inside).

\section{Monitoring, forecasting and preventing violations}
\label{sec:results}
\textbf{Agents and benchmarks.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised. An environment checker labels each action. We use four open agents, whose decision states we store: Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B and Muse-Glimmer-30B. We use two closed agents through their public APIs, with reasoning off: Claude Haiku 5.5 (Anthropic) and GPT-6-luna (OpenAI). The second benchmarks are AgentDojo (prompt injection) and $\tau^2$-bench airline.

\subsection{Monitoring at the call}
\label{sec:monitor}
\textbf{The read-out.} At each proposed call, the agent answers 8 yes/no questions about the call through its own API, 4 times each. The fixed read-out of \cref{sec:method} scores the answers. Nothing is fit and no label is used: we fixed the questions and the read-out on development data.

\textbf{It ranks violations about as well as a probe on the hidden states.} On held-out calls, the within-template AUROC is \GFtFixed\ (\cref{tab:g15}). A probe trained on the agent's true decision states, a comparison that needs the weights and labels, scores \GFtTrainedWB; the mean difference is \GFtTtwo. The read-out beats the agent's single self-audit answer by \GFtTone. It does not need the full decision state: the geometry of the 8 answers agrees with that state at only 0.01--0.16, and on open agents about 20\% [13, 27] of its ranking power runs through the hidden violation direction. The rest comes from the transcript, which every reader sees.

\textbf{It beats a reasoning judge on open agents.} Gemma-4, with reasoning on, read each conversation and gave one holistic violation probability. It ranked the open agents' violations worse than the read-out (read-out $-$ judge \GTwJone; two-sided test). Fixed questions and averaged angles beat one verdict. On Claude Haiku 5.5's actions, which it answered with its reasoning off, the judge is better (\cref{tab:g15}).

\textbf{With no label, the read-out tells where it works.} Its variance beyond its known sampling noise is its SNR (\cref{prop:snrceil}). Over \GCwCells\ agent--benchmark--stratum cells, the SNR predicts the AUROC (Spearman \GCwCone): cells with SNR $>0$ score \GCwPos, and cells with SNR $\le0$ score \GCwNeg. The same holds for each question: over eight agent--benchmark pairs, a question's label-free spread beyond its noise predicts how well it ranks violations (mean Spearman \GTfFone). Claude Haiku 5.5's settings have the lowest SNR: its four answers agree on 96--97\% of questions.

\textbf{At the call, another answerer does as well, as the theory implies.} The transcript and the call decide the violation (\cref{cor:action}), and the answers read a geometry that models share. So another model that answers the same questions about the same call should match the agent. It does on open agents (own $-$ other +0.012 [$-$0.018, 0.040]), and it does better on Claude Haiku 5.5 ($-$0.09 on restarts; $-$0.29 on $\tau^2$ with reasoning on). Claude Haiku 5.5 itself, reviewing its own calls with reasoning on, reaches 0.918 on $\tau^2$. Monitoring at the call thus works through the agent's own API, but it does not need the agent; forecasting does (\cref{sec:forecast}).

\subsection{Carrying to new benchmarks and closed agents}
\label{sec:carry}
\textbf{Prompt injection.} On AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, the violation is a call that carries out an instruction hidden in a tool output. We wrote 8 questions from that policy before any answer existed. The same read-out ranks the open agents' injected calls within suite, on held-out tasks with no defense, at \GSvGemma\ (Gemma-4, \GSvGemmaN) and \GSvGpt\ (gpt-oss-20b, \GSvGptN); the bar was a lower bound above 0.60. Here the single overall question is nearly as good (difference \GSvVtwo). Claude Haiku 5.5 almost never carried out these injections (none in 271 development episodes), so this benchmark has no closed-agent arm.

\textbf{GPT-6-luna.} Through its own API, its answers to the same 8 questions rank its CAPE-Bench violations within template and tool at \GGlAt\ (\GGlN). Its single answer scores 0.795.

\textbf{Claude Haiku 5.5 on CAPE-Bench.} The read-out ranks its unapproved restarts at \GFtClosedAFixed, where its single self-audit answer scores 0.32. Pooled over all its actions, the read-out scores 0.475: a fact that the agent never checked decides its mailing-list sends, so its state holds no warning about them.

\textbf{Claude Haiku 5.5 on $\tau^2$-bench.} On $\tau^2$-bench airline \citep{barres2025tau2}, Claude Haiku 5.5 breaks the published policy at some of its writes; for example, it cancels a booking that meets none of the conditions. We wrote 8 questions from the airline policy before any answer existed. The within-tool AUROC is \GEeRone\ (\GEeN, in 7 tasks; bar: lower bound above 0.5), and its single answer to whether the call complies scores \GEeSingle. With 5\% of writes reviewed, the read-out catches \GEeCaught. Per task (exploratory), it ranks the violations of 5 of the 7 tasks (33 of 50) at 0.77--1.00. In the other two tasks, the agent made an honest mistake: its own reply called the write eligible (it missed a flown date; it reversed a trip). Its state held no warning to read.

\subsection{Forecasting before the call}
\label{sec:forecast}
\textbf{The pre-call state.} Before each step's call, the agent answers 8 questions about its next action. The call is not shown, because it does not exist yet.

\textbf{GPT-6-luna's state forecasts the coming violation.} In a first run (exploratory), its pre-call answers ranked the coming violation at \GGlPre, no better than position alone (\GGlPos), but among calls at the same position they ranked it at \GGlPrePos. A preregistered replication on \GCfN\ confirms both: within template and tool \GCfTT\ (position alone 0.777), and among calls at the same position \GCfPos\ (9 strata holding 27 violations).

\textbf{Whose forecast is it?} Gemma-4 answered the same questions in GPT-6-luna's seat, on the same conversations. Within each kind of task and tool it forecast worse: 0.728, against 0.840 for GPT-6-luna's own answers (+0.111 [0.041, 0.185]). At the same position the difference, +0.08 [$-$0.02, 0.23], does not separate. On open agents the test failed: pooled own $-$ other $-$0.03 [$-$0.16, 0.08] (Qwen3.8 +0.21; gpt-oss-20b $-$0.16; Muse-Glimmer $-$0.15). \PENDING{fresh episodes of both closed agents: own $-$ other, within template and tool and within position}

\textbf{Open agents.} Among calls at the same step of the same kind of task, the pre-call answers rank the violating calls at 0.67 on Gemma-4 (exploratory) and at 0.669 [0.594, 0.764] pooled over three more agents (gpt-oss-20b 0.68, Qwen3.8 0.86, Muse-Glimmer 0.47). On the same steps, 16 answers per question instead of 4 raise the pooled value from 0.65 to 0.73. The deciding fact enters at the call's own step: the answers then rank the call better than one step earlier (+0.16 [0.03, 0.30]).

\textbf{Where it does not forecast.} Before Claude Haiku 5.5's $\tau^2$ writes, its answers did not rank the coming violation (0.54 [0.43, 0.68]). A Kalman forecast from earlier steps failed on Gemma-4 but beat position on three other agents (+0.21 [0.08, 0.32]).

\subsection{Preventing violations}
\label{sec:prevent-live}
\textbf{Self-policing.} Before a non-read call runs, a side branch asks the agent whether the call complies with its policy, and a flagged call goes to review. At one state, a gate prevents the share $\tau$ of violating calls that the agent flags. Over many states, it prevents the mean of $\tau$, weighted by where the violations occur (\cref{prop:prevention}).

\textbf{Live results.} On unseen scenarios (\cref{tab:selfpolice}), Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes and reached more goals. Claude Haiku 5.5, asked to check its policy rule by rule, prevented 69\% of the violating episodes that its transcript decides, at no cost in goals. Against prompt injection on AgentDojo, the gate cut attack success by 84\% and 50\%, at no cost in utility.

\textbf{The effect is predictable.} Offline flag rates, weighted by where each agent violates, predicted the live reduction of five agents within the registered 0.15 (reductions from 0.01 to 0.63; mean absolute error 0.035; \cref{fig:teaser}d). An earlier form of the law, with flag rates from another mix of tasks, failed (\cref{app:limits}).

\textbf{A gate on the pre-call state.} A first live test on GPT-6-luna compared gates at unequal review rates and did not separate them. We then ran a new test on fresh seeds with the gates at an equal review rate (\GLbRev\ reviews per episode). A gate on the pre-call read-out cut the episodes with a violation from \GLbNone\ to \GLbPre\ (relative reduction \GLbRed); a gate on position cut them to \GLbPos\ (difference \GLbPmP). Success did not change (\GLbSucc). The offline share of violating calls that the gate holds predicted its effect (\GLwPred; observed \GLwObs), but under-predicted the position gate (0.17 vs 0.44). On Gemma-4, whose pre-call read-out ranks weakly, the gate did not prevent. In all live tests, CAPE's checker stands in for the human reviewer.

\textbf{Trained comparisons} (\cref{app:trained}). With labels, a probe on an open reader of the transcript is stronger (0.98--0.99 on held-out actions of open and closed agents). But it needs labels for each policy, and it fell to 0.54 on unseen $\tau^2$ tasks. The training-free read-out needs neither, so the two are complementary.
