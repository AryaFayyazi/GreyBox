\section{Estimating the hidden geometry through the API}
\label{sec:geom-results}
We first test the reconstruction where the truth is visible: on open models, whose hidden states we store.
\textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (1{,}440 held out for confirmation) and 1{,}000 fresh contexts that add four kinds (\cref{app:details}); 88 questions; eight open chat models from seven families. Ground truth: each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at a layer chosen on development data (\cref{app:details}).

\textbf{Answers beat the input and the replies.} On every model, the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input does (gains \GOneGain; every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; \cref{fig:geometry}a, \cref{tab:geometry}). The model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, do little better than the input (\GFiveRep). Agreement rises with more questions (\GOneMore); 8 sampled answers per question keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts (400 of them of four new kinds), answers beat the input on 6 of 8 models. Against the strongest of five text baselines (three sentence embedders, on the input or the replies), answers win on 7 of 8 models on held-out contexts (gpt-oss ties: +0.013 [$-$0.020, 0.041]) and on 6 of 8 on fresh ones (Gemma-3 and gpt-oss lose by 0.10 and 0.15; exploratory). Raw $P(\textsc{yes})$ ranks about as well as the exact angles; what the angles add is a known noise, which the read-out's label-free ceiling uses (\cref{sec:monitor}).

\textbf{The geometry is shared across models.} A model's answers match another model's hidden geometry exactly as well as its own. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the mean own-model excess is \GSixExcess, inside the equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ that we fixed in advance (\cref{fig:geometry}b). So what the answers reveal is the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}. It also predicts the next section's result at the call: the shared part is what any model's answers can read.

\textbf{Which channel reveals what.} On 1{,}000 further fresh contexts, a preregistered ladder of API channels: sampled yes/no answers carry no own-model excess (+0.001 at the chosen layer; $-$0.006 at the last), while at the last layer the model-private part grows with the output channel (top-1 +0.056, top-20 +0.115, full distribution +0.112, log-probabilities +0.221 [0.211, 0.230]), and log-probabilities pick out the model itself in 7 of 8 cases, against 1 of 8 for answers. A closed API without log-probabilities therefore exposes the shared geometry of the state, and the agent's private part only through what it does (\cref{sec:forecast}).

\textbf{A short battery estimates a slice of the decision state.} By \cref{thm:geometry}(c), $m$ questions estimate the hidden geometry in the metric they pull back, a slice of rank at most $m$, not the full state. At agents' proposed calls, 8 policy questions agree with the full decision-state geometry at only 0.01--0.16; a linear map from the decision state to the answers, fit across scenarios with no label, gives the slice they pull back, and the answer geometry agrees with that slice at 0.60, 0.36, 0.38 and 0.58 on the four open agents, against 0.02--0.10 for random slices of the same rank (all 4 lower bounds above both; secondary: registered on existing tables). The battery reads a specific part of the state, and that part is what the monitor uses.

\textbf{A closed model reveals the same shared geometry.} Claude Haiku 5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of its answers give the same geometry (Spearman \GTwoSplit; bound fixed in advance: 0.7),
far more reproducible than its agreement with the input embedding (0.52) explains (\GTwoText). Its answers agree with open models' hidden geometries at \GTwoOpen.

\textbf{Its own hidden geometry, by triangulation.} If, as on open models, no part of its answers is specific to its own state, triangulation (\cref{prop:triang}) estimates its own agreement at 0.49 [0.44, 0.55]. Leaving each open model out, it predicts the true agreement to within \GVtMae\ on held-out contexts and 0.114 on fresh ones (\cref{app:prereg}).

\section{Monitoring, forecasting and preventing violations}
\label{sec:results}
\textbf{Agents and benchmarks.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised; its checker labels each action. Open agents (decision states stored): Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B, Muse-Glimmer-30B. Closed agents, through their APIs, reasoning off: Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6-luna. External benchmarks: AgentDojo and Sierra's $\tau^2$-bench airline.

\subsection{Monitoring at the call}
\label{sec:monitor}
\textbf{The read-out.} At each proposed call, the agent answers 8 yes/no questions about the call through its own API, 4 times each (\cref{app:questions}). The fixed read-out (\cref{eq:readout}; questions fixed on development data, no labels) scores the answers.

\textbf{It ranks violations about as well as a probe on the hidden states.} On held-out calls, the within-template AUROC is \GFtFixed\ (\cref{tab:g15}). A probe trained on the agent's true decision states, a comparison that needs the weights and labels, scores \GFtTrainedWB; the mean difference is \GFtTtwo. The read-out beats the agent's single self-audit answer by \GFtTone. On the open agents, it also beats a Gemma-4 judge that reasons, by \GTwJone. On Claude Haiku 5.5, whose answers we take with reasoning off, the judge is better (\cref{tab:g15}). The read-out reads the slice of the state its questions pull back (\cref{sec:geom-results}), not the full state: on open agents about 20\% [13, 27] of its ranking power runs through the hidden violation direction, the rest through the transcript, which any monitor can see.

\textbf{With no label, the read-out's SNR predicts where it works.} This is where the geometry's coordinates pay off: their sampling noise is known (\cref{thm:geometry}), so the read-out's variance beyond that noise, its SNR, needs no label (\cref{prop:snrceil}). Over 29 cells (agent, benchmark and stratum), the SNR predicts the AUROC (Spearman 0.71): cells with SNR $>0$ score 0.96 on average, cells with SNR $\le0$ score 0.68 (the nominal noise is conservative, so a low SNR flags a setting without ruling it out).

\textbf{At the call, another answerer does as well, as the theory implies.} The transcript decides the violation (\cref{cor:action}) and the answers read a geometry that models share, so another model answering about the same call should match the agent: on open agents it does (own $-$ other +0.012 [$-$0.018, 0.040]), and on Claude Haiku 5.5 it does better ($-$0.09 on restarts; $-$0.28 on $\tau^2$ with reasoning on). Reasoning may help at the call: Claude reviewing its own $\tau^2$ writes with reasoning on reaches 0.918 on 180 writes, against 0.699 for our read-out with reasoning off (difference not significant).

\textbf{Other benchmarks and closed agents.}\label{sec:carry} On AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, 8 questions written from the injection policy before any answer existed rank the open agents' injected calls at \GSvGemma\ and \GSvGpt. GPT-6-luna's answers rank its violations at \GGlAt. Claude Haiku 5.5's rank its unapproved restarts at \GFtClosedAFixed, but pooled score 0.475: a fact it never checked decides its mailing-list sends; on $\tau^2$-bench, \GEeRone.

\subsection{Forecasting before the call}
\label{sec:forecast}
\input{tab_forecast}
\textbf{The pre-call state.} Before each call, the agent answers 8 questions about its next action (its self-report). \Cref{tab:forecast} compares every forecaster on the same calls, at the same step, which removes the call's position.

\textbf{When text suffices: GPT-6-luna.} Its words track its actions: its self-report (0.84) and a text monitor (0.78) forecast as well as its sampled actions (0.73). We had registered the opposite expectation; that test failed, and we read this regime only after the data.

\textbf{When text does not suffice: Claude Haiku 5.5.} Claude Haiku 5.5 rarely violates (2 of 24 CAPE-Bench templates; 1.5--1.6\% on AgentDojo and $\tau^2$), and on one template it violates nearly always; its tests use the other, unapproved production restarts (41 scenarios), with exact contexts.

Among calls at the same step, its self-report and both reasoning text monitors are near chance or weak; only its sampled next actions forecast which call violates (\cref{tab:forecast}; own $-$ self-report \GSeDiff; own $-$ GPT-6-luna's judge \GXjDiff). Its words are nearly constant: before every violating call, each answer to whether it would comply said yes, and before 93\% (94\% fresh) of them none of its 32 answers admitted a violation; its rare admissions came before safe calls (descriptive). Gemma-4 sampling from Claude's contexts does not (0.54 at the same step).

Across steps, Gemma-4's draws tie Claude's own (0.82 vs 0.80; exploratory; the registered test on rebuilt contexts found Gemma-4's draws better, $-$0.14 [$-$0.20, $-$0.09]), and Claude's draws alone (0.80) score below the step alone (0.82): across steps, other readers track \emph{when} Claude violates; within a step, only its own policy tells \emph{which} call (+0.35 [0.28, 0.42]; exploratory). The sampled actions still add to the best text monitor across steps (0.90 to 0.96). 

\textbf{The registered results replicated.} On 492 fresh episodes (\GRpN\ calls), in a test registered with its Holm correction before any episode existed, all 8 endpoints passed: own draws $-$ self-report \GRpOne\ (primary), $-$ the two text monitors \GRpTwo\ and \GRpThree, lift across steps \GRpFour\ (the lift was first found on existing data, secondary). 

The policy read costs \$0.002 per decision (median 9\,s). On $\tau^2$-bench the forecast was not above chance (0.61 [0.44, 0.84]).

\textbf{Sampling the policy is what matters, not the reader.} GPT-6-luna reading Claude's sampled calls forecasts better than from the transcript (\GOrDone; fresh episodes \GRpSeven) and better than Claude's own reads (the registered equivalence of the readers failed).

\textbf{The geometry alone forecasts.} With no label and no question sign, the mean distance of the agent's sampled next actions from its typical state in the estimated geometry forecasts its coming violation: 0.89 on Claude Haiku 5.5 (0.87 on fresh episodes, registered before they existed), weakly 0.64 on GPT-6-luna, and on average 0.75 [0.68, 0.81] over four open agents across their task families (Gemma-4 0.77, gpt-oss-20b 0.75, Qwen3.8 0.91, Muse-Glimmer 0.56 [0.44, 0.68]; secondary: on existing draws).

\textbf{What the weights add.} A probe on CAPE's stored decision state forecasts better: the registered comparison failed, the probe leading by 0.16 [0.04, 0.28] (Gemma-4 0.97 vs 0.78; gpt-oss-20b 0.82 vs 0.70). That state is read after the agent's own hidden reasoning for the turn. On Gemma-4, a probe at the start of the turn, the moment a closed API forecasts from, scores 0.80 on 217 units, against 0.78 for the API geometry and 0.80 for the signed API forecast; turn-start minus after-reasoning probe is $-$0.20 [$-$0.31, $-$0.10]. So on this agent most of the white box's lead may come from the reasoning, but the registered non-inferiority test at turn start ($-$0.02 [$-$0.16, 0.12], bar $-$0.05) was too small to pass; a powered test is under way.

\textbf{The reconstructed geometry alone flags violations.} Before each step, both closed agents drew calls in side branches, and CAPE's checker labelled them. With no label and no question sign, a drawn call's Fisher--Rao distance from the agent's typical state ranks the violating ones: \GGeoClaude\ on Claude Haiku 5.5 (1{,}765 calls, restarts only, so within tool) and \GGeoGpt\ on GPT-6-luna (832 calls; the interval is wide; secondary). The signed read-out of \cref{sec:monitor} is one projection of this geometry; it adds the direction of each question and scores higher (0.89 and 0.99 on the same calls). On the fresh episodes, the geometric monitor scored 0.81.

\textbf{The theory predicts the loss of any forecaster that does not sample.} By \cref{prop:selfforecast}, a forecast $q$ from the context alone loses $\E(f-q)^2$ in Brier score against the agent's policy $f$; the agent's draws, checked by CAPE's checker, estimate this without a label on the executed call (\cref{cor:rollout}). Registered before any episode existed, the predicted loss of another model's draws matched the observed on both closed agents (a first Claude test, with contexts rebuilt from logs, failed: $-$0.046): 0.122 vs 0.126 on Claude Haiku 5.5 (difference \mbox{+0.004} [$-$0.003, 0.010]) and 0.018 vs 0.020 on GPT-6-luna (+0.002 [$-$0.004, 0.008]; bar $\pm0.02$). The predicted losses of three text monitors also matched (each inside $\pm0.02$; secondary), and on the fresh episodes they matched again (\GRpSixA\ and \GRpSixB). The prediction needs checked draws: with GPT-6-luna's judgments in place of the checker it failed, even after recalibration. In Brier terms the two agents' text monitors lose about equally, so the registered Brier separation of the two regimes failed: GPT-6-luna's monitor ranks well but is poorly calibrated.

\subsection{Preventing violations}
\label{sec:prevent-live}
\textbf{Self-policing.} Before a non-read call runs, a side branch asks the agent whether it complies with its policy; a flagged call goes to review. A gate prevents the share of violating calls that the agent flags, averaged over where violations occur (\cref{prop:prevention}).

\textbf{Live results} (CAPE's checker reviewing held calls; \cref{tab:selfpolice}). Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes; Claude Haiku 5.5, checking its policy rule by rule, 69\% of those its transcript decides (31\% over all scenarios); against prompt injection on AgentDojo, attack success fell by 84\% and 50\%; goals and utility were not lost.

\textbf{The effect is predictable.} In a preregistered test, offline flag rates, weighted by where each agent violates, predicted the live reduction of five agents within the registered 0.15 (mean absolute error 0.035; \cref{fig:law}). An earlier form, with flag rates from another mix of tasks, failed, and on AgentDojo the law missed Gemma-4 by 0.16.

\textbf{A gate on the pre-call state.} On fresh GPT-6-luna episodes, at an equal review rate (checker as reviewer), a gate on the pre-call read-out cut violating episodes from 31.7\% to 7.9\%, a gate on the step to 17.9\% (\GLbPmP; success 18.7\% vs 27.8\% with the step gate). The law predicted the first exactly (0.75) but under-predicted the step gate (0.17 vs 0.44); an earlier design at unequal rates did not separate the gates, and on Gemma-4 a pre-call gate did not prevent.

\textbf{Prevention with no reviewer.} When the agent's own read-out flags a call, the call is held once and the agent is told to re-check; an identical re-proposal runs. On Claude Haiku 5.5's restarts, a first test (492 fresh units) cut episodes with a violation from \GSgNone\ to \GSgSelf, against \GSgRand\ for random holds. But random holds ran at a lower dose (\GSgHold\ holds per call, self and random). A replication on fresh seeds set the random rate from that test's data to match the dose (0.21 and 0.23 holds per call). It confirmed the result: violating episodes fell from 67.9\% to 35.2\%, against 42.9\% for random holds (self $-$ random reduction +0.114 [0.035, 0.200]). Success rose by +0.081 [0.039, 0.130] (random holds: +0.098). All three Holm endpoints survive. 

On GPT-6-luna (six templates) it cut them from \GSgGNone\ to \GSgGSelf, against \GSgGRand\ for random holds (\GSgGDiff; success \GSgGSucc, lower bound narrowly below the registered $-0.05$), mostly on the template the transcript decides; where an unchecked fact decides it, nothing changed, as \cref{cor:action} predicts. 

A policy reminder, with nothing held, prevented more than holds did: timed by the sampled forecast it cut violating episodes to \GFsFc, and at random turns to \GFsRand\ (no detected difference), with success rising from 13\% to 40\%. On this template, the reminder's text does most of the work, and the forecast's timing adds little.
