\section{Reconstructing the hidden geometry from answers}
\label{sec:geom-results}
We first test the reconstruction where the truth is visible: on open models, whose hidden states we store.
\textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (1{,}440 held out for confirmation) and 1{,}000 fresh contexts that add four kinds (news, programming, jailbreaks, prompt injections); 88 questions; eight open chat models from seven families. Ground truth: each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at a layer chosen on development data (\cref{app:details}).

(b) Answers of model A against the hidden geometry of model B on 1{,}000 fresh contexts: the own-model diagonal (outlined) is not special.}

\textbf{Answers beat the input and the replies.} On every model, the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input does (gains \GOneGain; every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; \cref{fig:geometry}a, \cref{tab:geometry}). The model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, do little better than the input (\GFiveRep). Agreement rises with more questions (\GOneMore); 8 sampled answers per question keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts (400 of them of four new kinds), answers beat the input on 6 of 8 models. Against the strongest of five text baselines (three sentence embedders, on the input or the replies), answers win on 7 of 8 models on held-out contexts (gpt-oss ties: +0.013 [$-$0.020, 0.041]) and on 6 of 8 on fresh ones (Gemma-3 and gpt-oss lose by 0.10 and 0.15; exploratory). Raw $P(\textsc{yes})$ ranks about as well as the exact angles; what the angles add is a known noise, which the read-out's label-free ceiling uses (\cref{sec:monitor}).

\textbf{The geometry is shared across models.} A model's answers match another model's hidden geometry exactly as well as its own. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the mean own-model excess is \GSixExcess, inside the equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ that we fixed in advance (\cref{fig:geometry}b). So what the answers reveal is the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}. It also predicts the next section's result at the call: the shared part is what any model's answers can read.

\textbf{A closed model reveals the same shared geometry.} Claude Haiku 5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of its answers give the same geometry (Spearman \GTwoSplit; bound fixed in advance: 0.7),
far more reproducible than its agreement with the input embedding (0.52) explains (\GTwoText). Its answers agree with open models' hidden geometries at \GTwoOpen.

\textbf{Its own hidden geometry, by triangulation.} If, as on open models, no part of its answers is specific to its own state, triangulation (\cref{prop:triang}) estimates its own agreement at 0.49 ([0.44, 0.55]; [0.32, 0.53] over open models' loadings). Leaving each open model out, it predicts the true agreement to within \GVtMae\ (\GVtInside\ of 8 inside the interval); the mean of the other open models does as well (0.053), but gives every closed model the same value. On the 1{,}000 fresh contexts it does worse (error 0.114; 4 of 8 inside).

\section{Monitoring, forecasting and preventing violations}
\label{sec:results}
\textbf{Agents and benchmarks.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised; its checker labels each action. Open agents (decision states stored): Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B, Muse-Glimmer-30B. Closed agents, through their APIs, reasoning off: Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6-luna. External benchmarks: AgentDojo and Sierra's $\tau^2$-bench airline.

\subsection{Monitoring at the call}
\label{sec:monitor}
\textbf{The read-out.} At each proposed call, the agent answers 8 yes/no questions about the call through its own API, 4 times each (\cref{app:questions}). The fixed read-out (\cref{eq:readout}; questions fixed on development data, no labels) scores the answers.

\textbf{It ranks violations about as well as a probe on the hidden states.} On held-out calls, the within-template AUROC is \GFtFixed\ (\cref{tab:g15}). A probe trained on the agent's true decision states, a comparison that needs the weights and labels, scores \GFtTrainedWB; the mean difference is \GFtTtwo. The read-out beats the agent's single self-audit answer by \GFtTone. On the open agents, it also beats a Gemma-4 judge that reasons, by \GTwJone. On Claude Haiku 5.5, whose answers we take with reasoning off, the judge is better (\cref{tab:g15}). The read-out does not need the full decision state: the geometry of the 8 answers agrees with that state at only 0.01--0.16. On open agents, about 20\% [13, 27] of its ranking power runs through the hidden violation direction. The rest runs through the transcript, which any monitor can see.

\textbf{With no label, the read-out's SNR predicts where it works.} This is where the geometry's coordinates pay off: their sampling noise is known (\cref{thm:geometry}), so the read-out's variance beyond that noise, its SNR, needs no label (\cref{prop:snrceil}). Over 29 cells (agent, benchmark and stratum), the SNR predicts the AUROC (Spearman 0.71): cells with SNR $>0$ score 0.96 on average, cells with SNR $\le0$ score 0.68 (the nominal noise is conservative, so a low SNR flags a setting without ruling it out).

\textbf{At the call, another answerer does as well, as the theory implies.} The transcript and the call decide the violation (\cref{cor:action}), and the answers read a geometry that models share. So another model that answers the same questions about the same call should match the agent. On open agents it matches (own $-$ other +0.012 [$-$0.018, 0.040]). On Claude Haiku 5.5 the other model does better (own $-$ other $-$0.09 on restarts; $-$0.28 on $\tau^2$ with reasoning on). Reasoning helps at the call. With reasoning on, Claude Haiku 5.5 reviewing its own $\tau^2$ writes reaches 0.918 (180 writes). Our read-out reaches 0.699 on the same writes, but it runs with reasoning off for cost. Monitoring at the call thus works through the agent's own API, but it does not need the agent; before the call, the forecast is the agent's policy (\cref{sec:forecast}).

\subsection{Carrying to new benchmarks and closed agents}
\label{sec:carry}
\textbf{Prompt injection and GPT-6-luna.} On AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, where the violation is a call that carries out an instruction hidden in a tool output, 8 questions written from that policy before any answer existed rank the open agents' injected calls within suite at \GSvGemma\ (Gemma-4) and \GSvGpt\ (gpt-oss-20b). Through its own API, GPT-6-luna's answers rank its CAPE-Bench violations within template and tool at \GGlAt\ (single answer 0.795).

\textbf{Claude Haiku 5.5.} On CAPE-Bench, the read-out ranks its unapproved restarts at \GFtClosedAFixed\ (single self-audit answer 0.32), but pooled it scores 0.475: a fact it never checked decides its mailing-list sends. On $\tau^2$-bench, with 8 questions written from the airline policy before any answer existed, it scores \GEeRone\ within tool (\GEeN; single answer \GEeSingle); in two of 7 tasks the agent misread the booking.

\subsection{Forecasting before the call}
\label{sec:forecast}
\input{tab_forecast}
\textbf{The pre-call state.} Before each call, the agent answers 8 questions about its next action (its self-report). \Cref{tab:forecast} compares every forecaster on the same calls, at the same step, which removes the call's position.

\textbf{When text suffices: GPT-6-luna.} Its words track its actions. On 504 new episodes, its self-report (0.84) and a text monitor (0.78) forecast as well as its sampled actions (0.73). We had registered the opposite expectation, that its sampled actions would win; that test failed, and we read it as this regime only after the data. An earlier own-versus-other advantage did not replicate.

\textbf{When text does not suffice: Claude Haiku 5.5.} Claude Haiku 5.5 rarely violates (2 of 24 CAPE-Bench templates, 1.6\% of AgentDojo injections, 1.5\% of $\tau^2$ episodes), and on one of the two templates it violates in nearly every episode. Its tests therefore use unapproved production restarts (41 scenarios), with the exact context of each call.

Among calls at the same step, its self-report and both reasoning text monitors are near chance or weak; only its sampled next actions forecast which call violates (\cref{tab:forecast}; own $-$ self-report \GSeDiff; own $-$ GPT-6-luna's judge \GXjDiff). Gemma-4 sampling from Claude's contexts does not (0.54 at the same step).

Across steps, Gemma-4's draws tie Claude's own (0.82 vs 0.80; exploratory), and the registered own-versus-other test across steps failed ($-$0.14 with rebuilt contexts). So another model's policy tracks \emph{when} Claude violates; only Claude's own policy tells \emph{which} call (+0.35 [0.28, 0.42] within a step; exploratory). Across steps, Claude's draws alone (0.80) score below the step alone (0.82), and the text monitors also track \emph{when}. Still, the sampled actions add to the best text monitor, with no fit (0.90 to 0.96, \cref{tab:forecast}). 

\textbf{The registered results replicated.} Before any fresh episode existed, we registered a test with 8 endpoints and fixed their Holm correction. On 492 fresh episodes (\GRpN\ calls, \GRpViol\ violations), all 8 passed and survived the correction. Own draws minus self-report: \GRpOne\ (primary). Own draws minus the two text monitors: \GRpTwo\ and \GRpThree. Lift across steps: \GRpFour. 

Per decision, the policy read costs \$0.002 (median 9\,s), the text monitor \$0.0004 (3.5\,s). On $\tau^2$-bench airline the forecast was not above chance (0.61 [0.44, 0.84], 7 tasks): Claude's draws mostly repeat the write.

\textbf{Sampling the policy is what matters, not the reader.} GPT-6-luna reading Claude's sampled calls forecasts better than it does from the transcript (\GOrDone), and better than Claude's own reads, so the registered equivalence of the two readers failed. With no label and no question sign, the draws' mean distance from the agent's typical state in the reconstructed geometry also forecasts (0.89 for Claude, 0.87 on the fresh episodes; 0.64 for GPT-6-luna). On the fresh episodes, GPT-6-luna reading the draws again beat reading the transcript (\GRpSeven).

\textbf{The reconstructed geometry alone flags violations.} Before each step, both closed agents drew calls in side branches, and CAPE's checker labelled them. With no label and no question sign, a drawn call's Fisher--Rao distance from the agent's typical state ranks the violating ones: \GGeoClaude\ on Claude Haiku 5.5 (1{,}765 calls, restarts only, so within tool) and \GGeoGpt\ on GPT-6-luna (832 calls, within template and tool; the interval is wide). The signed read-out of \cref{sec:monitor} is one projection of this geometry; it adds the direction of each question and scores higher (0.89 and 0.99 on the same calls). On the fresh episodes, the geometric monitor scored 0.81.

\textbf{The theory predicts the loss of any forecaster that does not sample.} By \cref{prop:selfforecast}, a forecast $q$ from the context alone loses $\E(f-q)^2$ in Brier score against the agent's policy $f$; the agent's draws, checked by CAPE's checker, estimate this without a label on the executed call (\cref{cor:rollout}). Registered before any episode existed, the predicted loss of another model's draws matched the observed on both closed agents: 0.122 vs 0.126 on Claude Haiku 5.5 (difference \mbox{+0.004} [$-$0.003, 0.010]) and 0.018 vs 0.020 on GPT-6-luna (+0.002 [$-$0.004, 0.008]; bar $\pm0.02$). The predicted losses of three text monitors also matched (each inside $\pm0.02$), and on the fresh episodes they matched again (\GRpSixA\ and \GRpSixB). The prediction needs checked draws: with GPT-6-luna's judgments in place of the checker it failed, even after recalibration. In Brier terms the two agents' text monitors lose about equally, so the registered Brier separation of the two regimes failed: GPT-6-luna's monitor ranks well but is poorly calibrated.

\subsection{Preventing violations}
\label{sec:prevent-live}
\textbf{Self-policing.} Before a non-read call runs, a side branch asks the agent whether it complies with its policy; a flagged call goes to review. A gate prevents the share of violating calls that the agent flags, averaged over where violations occur (\cref{prop:prevention}).

\textbf{Live results.} On unseen scenarios (\cref{tab:selfpolice}), Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes and reached more goals. Claude Haiku 5.5, asked to check its policy rule by rule, prevented 69\% of the violating episodes that its transcript decides (2.8\% of its episodes; 31\% over all scenarios), at no cost in goals. Against prompt injection on AgentDojo, the gate cut attack success by 84\% and 50\%, at no cost in utility.

\textbf{The effect is predictable.} In a preregistered test, offline flag rates, weighted by where each agent violates, predicted the live reduction of five agents within the registered 0.15 (mean absolute error 0.035; \cref{fig:law}). An earlier form, with flag rates from another mix of tasks, failed, and on AgentDojo the law missed Gemma-4 by 0.16.

\textbf{A gate on the pre-call state.} Live on fresh GPT-6-luna episodes, at an equal review rate (CAPE's checker as reviewer), a gate on the pre-call read-out cut violating episodes from 31.7\% to 7.9\%, a gate on the call's step to 17.9\% (difference in relative reduction \GLbPmP; success 18.7\% with the pre-call gate, as with no gate, and 27.8\% with the step gate). The law predicted the first (0.75 vs 0.75) but not the second (0.17 vs 0.44); a first design at unequal rates did not separate the gates, and on Gemma-4 a pre-call gate did not prevent.

\textbf{Prevention with no reviewer.} When the agent's own read-out flags a call, the call is held once and the agent is told to re-check; an identical re-proposal runs. On Claude Haiku 5.5's restarts, a first test (492 fresh units) cut episodes with a violation from \GSgNone\ to \GSgSelf, against \GSgRand\ for random holds. But random holds ran at a lower dose (\GSgHold\ holds per call, self and random). A replication on fresh seeds set the random rate from that test's data to match the dose (0.21 and 0.23 holds per call). It confirmed the result: violating episodes fell from 67.9\% to 35.2\%, against 42.9\% for random holds (self $-$ random reduction +0.114 [0.035, 0.200]). Success rose by +0.081 [0.039, 0.130] (random holds: +0.098). All endpoints survive the Holm correction. 

On GPT-6-luna (six templates, 252 units per arm), it cut them from \GSgGNone\ to \GSgGSelf, against \GSgGRand\ for random holds, which held more calls (difference \GSgGDiff); success changed by \GSgGSucc, the lower bound narrowly missing the registered $-0.05$. Most of its effect comes from one template, where the transcript decides the violation (45\% to 0\% of episodes); where an unchecked fact decides it, nothing changed, as \cref{cor:action} predicts. 

A policy reminder, with nothing held, prevented more than holds did: timed by the sampled forecast it cut violating episodes to \GFsFc, and at random turns to \GFsRand\ (no detected difference), with success rising from 13\% to 40\%. On this template, the reminder's text does most of the work, and the forecast's timing adds little.
