\section{Reconstructing the hidden geometry from answers}
\label{sec:geom-results}
We first test the reconstruction where the truth is visible: on open models, whose hidden states we store.
\textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (3{,}360 development, 1{,}440 confirmation). Two more sets of 1{,}000 fresh contexts add four new kinds: news, programming, jailbreaks and prompt injections. We ask 88 questions about the message, on eight open chat models from seven families. The ground truth is each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at a layer chosen on development data. All results are on held-out contexts (\cref{app:details}).

(b) Answers of model A against the hidden geometry of model B on 1{,}000 fresh contexts: the own-model diagonal (outlined) is not special.}
\label{fig:geometry}

\textbf{Answers beat the input and the replies.} On every model, the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input does (gains \GOneGain; every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; \cref{fig:geometry}a, \cref{tab:geometry}). The model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, do little better than the input (\GFiveRep). Agreement rises with the number of questions (from 8 to 88: \GOneMore), and 8 sampled answers per question keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts of four new kinds, answers beat the input on 6 of 8 models. Raw $P(\textsc{yes})$ ranks about as well as the exact angles; what the angles add is a known noise, which the read-out's label-free ceiling uses (\cref{sec:monitor}).

\textbf{The geometry is shared across models.} A model's answers match another model's hidden geometry exactly as well as its own. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the mean own-model excess is \GSixExcess, inside the equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ that we fixed in advance (\cref{fig:geometry}b); the hidden geometries of different models also agree with each other (\GSixHH). So what the answers reveal is the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}. This finding drives the rest of the paper: the shared part is what any model's answers can read.

\textbf{A closed model reveals the same shared geometry.} Claude Haiku 5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of its answers give the same geometry (Spearman \GTwoSplit; bound fixed in advance: 0.7), which carries \GTwoText\ beyond the input embedding. Its answers agree with open models' hidden geometries at \GTwoOpen.

\textbf{Its own hidden geometry, by triangulation.} Its hidden states are not visible. If, as on open models, no part of its answers is specific to its own state, triangulation (\cref{prop:triang}) estimates its own agreement at 0.49 (bootstrap interval [0.44, 0.55]; [0.32, 0.53] over the range of open models' loadings). Leaving each open model out as if closed, the same estimate predicts the true agreement to within \GVtMae\ on average (\GVtInside\ of 8 inside the predicted interval); a model-blind estimate, the mean of the other open models, does as well (0.053), but it gives every closed model the same value, while triangulation uses the closed model's own answers.

\section{Monitoring, forecasting and preventing violations}
\label{sec:results}
This section tests monitoring, forecasting and prevention: at the call, a fixed read-out monitors as well as a probe and carries to new benchmarks and closed agents (\cref{sec:monitor,sec:carry}); before the call, the forecast is the agent's own policy (\cref{sec:forecast}); and gates on these reads prevent violations by a predictable amount (\cref{sec:prevent-live}).

\textbf{Agents and benchmarks.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised. An environment checker labels each action. We use four open agents, whose decision states we store: Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B and Muse-Glimmer-30B. We use two closed agents through their public APIs, with reasoning off: Claude Haiku 5.5 (Anthropic) and GPT-6-luna (OpenAI). Two external benchmarks complete the set: AgentDojo (prompt injection) and Sierra's $\tau^2$-bench airline; GPT-6-luna's results span six CAPE-Bench templates.

\subsection{Monitoring at the call}
\label{sec:monitor}
\textbf{The read-out.} At each proposed call, the agent answers 8 yes/no questions about the call through its own API, 4 times each (\cref{app:questions}). The fixed read-out (\cref{eq:readout}; questions fixed on development data, no labels) scores the answers.

\textbf{It ranks violations about as well as a probe on the hidden states.} On held-out calls, the within-template AUROC is \GFtFixed\ (\cref{tab:g15}). A probe trained on the agent's true decision states, a comparison that needs the weights and labels, scores \GFtTrainedWB; the mean difference is \GFtTtwo. The read-out beats the agent's single self-audit answer by \GFtTone. It does not need the full decision state: the geometry of the 8 answers agrees with that state at only 0.01--0.16, and on open agents about 20\% [13, 27] of its ranking power runs through the hidden violation direction. The rest comes from the transcript, which every reader sees.

\textbf{It beats a reasoning judge on open agents.} Gemma-4 with reasoning on, giving one holistic violation probability per conversation, ranked the open agents' violations worse than the read-out (read-out $-$ judge \GTwJone); on Claude Haiku 5.5's actions, answered with reasoning off, the judge is better (\cref{tab:g15}).

\textbf{With no label, the read-out tells where it works.} This is where the geometry's coordinates pay off: their sampling noise is known (\cref{thm:geometry}), so the read-out's variance beyond that noise, its SNR, needs no label (\cref{prop:snrceil}). Over 29 cells (agent, benchmark and stratum), the SNR predicts the AUROC (Spearman 0.71): cells with SNR $>0$ score 0.96 on average, cells with SNR $\le0$ score 0.68 (the nominal noise is conservative, so a low SNR flags a setting without ruling it out). The same holds question by question (mean Spearman 0.58). Claude Haiku 5.5 has the lowest SNR: its four answers agree on 96--97\% of questions.

\textbf{At the call, another answerer does as well, as the theory implies.} The transcript and the call decide the violation (\cref{cor:action}), and the answers read a geometry that models share. So another model that answers the same questions about the same call should match the agent. It does on open agents (own $-$ other +0.012 [$-$0.018, 0.040]), and it does better on Claude Haiku 5.5 ($-$0.09 on restarts; $-$0.29 on $\tau^2$ with reasoning on). Monitoring at the call thus works through the agent's own API, but it does not need the agent; before the call, the forecast is the agent's policy (\cref{sec:forecast}).

\subsection{Carrying to new benchmarks and closed agents}
\label{sec:carry}
\textbf{Prompt injection.} On AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, the violation is a call that carries out an instruction hidden in a tool output. With 8 questions written from that policy before any answer existed, the same read-out ranks the open agents' injected calls within suite, on held-out tasks with no defense, at \GSvGemma\ (Gemma-4) and \GSvGpt\ (gpt-oss-20b).

\textbf{GPT-6-luna.} Through its own API, its answers to the same 8 questions rank its CAPE-Bench violations within template and tool at \GGlAt\ (\GGlN). Its single answer scores 0.795.

\textbf{Claude Haiku 5.5 on CAPE-Bench.} The read-out ranks its unapproved restarts at \GFtClosedAFixed, where its single self-audit answer scores 0.32. Pooled over all its actions, the read-out scores 0.475: a fact that the agent never checked decides its mailing-list sends, so its state holds no warning about them.

\textbf{Claude Haiku 5.5 on $\tau^2$-bench.} On $\tau^2$-bench airline \citep{barres2025tau2}, Claude Haiku 5.5 breaks the published policy at some of its writes. With 8 questions written from the airline policy before any answer existed, the within-tool AUROC is \GEeRone\ (\GEeN; single answer \GEeSingle). Per task (exploratory), it ranks the violations of 5 of the 7 tasks at 0.77--1.00; in the other two the agent misread the booking, so its state held no warning.

\subsection{Forecasting before the call}
\label{sec:forecast}
\textbf{The pre-call state.} Before each step's call, the agent answers 8 questions about its next action. The call does not exist yet, so no reader of the call's text can help, and comparing calls at the same step removes the call's position.

\textbf{GPT-6-luna's state forecasts the coming violation.} On 252 fresh episodes (1{,}047 calls, 68 violations), its pre-call answers ranked the coming violation at 0.84 within template and tool, where position alone scores 0.78, and at 0.92 among calls at the same position (9 strata, 27 violations). A second fresh set of 504 episodes over six templates (\GFfGptN) gave \GFfGptPos\ [0.673, 0.939] among calls at the same position (the per-agent value of a preregistered pooled test).

\textbf{Whose forecast is it?} By \cref{prop:selfforecast}, another reader loses only as far as its policy differs from the agent's. Gemma-4, answering the same questions in GPT-6-luna's seat on the second set, forecast as well (\GFfGptOthTT\ vs \GFfGptOwnTT\ within template and tool): it reads GPT-6-luna's next move as GPT-6-luna does, so the preregistered claim that the agent's own answers forecast better, which an earlier test on GPT-6-luna had supported (+0.111 [0.041, 0.185]), failed on new episodes of both closed agents. Claude Haiku 5.5 is the opposite case: asked about its next action, its answers point the wrong way (\GFfClaudeOwnTT), so its self-report is not its policy; Gemma-4 in its seat does better within template and tool (0.742), but mostly through position (0.522 within position).

\textbf{The forecast lives in the policy, not the self-report.} \Cref{prop:selfforecast} says the forecast is the agent's own policy, so it can also be read by sampling (\cref{cor:rollout}): draw the agent's next action 8 times in side branches and read each drawn call with its own answers. On 492 fresh Claude Haiku 5.5 episodes (\GRoN), its own draws rank the coming violation among calls at the same position at \GRoOwn, where its self-report scores \GRoReport\ (difference \GRoOwnMinusReport; \cref{fig:saysdoes}). Asked what it will do, it does not say; sampled, it does. Gemma-4 sampling in its seat does not forecast at the same position (\GRoOther); pooled over positions its draws score \GRoOtherTT\ because they track the step at which Claude violates, so the registered comparison, within template and tool, failed. The two policies differ (policy gap \GPgGap), but the loss that \cref{prop:selfforecast} predicts for Gemma-4 (0.102) exceeded the observed loss (0.056): Claude's draws were miscalibrated (0.17 vs 0.21) because our replay of its history dropped refused extra tool calls. \PENDING{the same test with the exact context}

\subsection{Preventing violations}
\label{sec:prevent-live}
\textbf{Self-policing.} Before a non-read call runs, a side branch asks the agent whether the call complies with its policy, and a flagged call goes to review. At one state, a gate prevents the share $\tau$ of violating calls that the agent flags. Over many states, it prevents the mean of $\tau$, weighted by where the violations occur (\cref{prop:prevention}).

\textbf{Live results.} On unseen scenarios (\cref{tab:selfpolice}), Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes and reached more goals. Claude Haiku 5.5, asked to check its policy rule by rule, prevented 69\% of the violating episodes that its transcript decides (2.8\% of its episodes; 31\% over all scenarios), at no cost in goals. Against prompt injection on AgentDojo, the gate cut attack success by 84\% and 50\%, at no cost in utility.

\textbf{The effect is predictable.} In a preregistered test, offline flag rates, weighted by where each agent violates, predicted the live reduction of five agents within the registered 0.15 (reductions from 0.01 to 0.63; mean absolute error 0.035; \cref{fig:teaser}d). An earlier form, with flag rates from another mix of tasks, failed, and on AgentDojo the law missed Gemma-4 by 0.16.

\textbf{A gate on the pre-call state.} Live, on fresh GPT-6-luna episodes, we compared two gates at an equal review rate (about 1.5 reviews per episode). A gate on the pre-call read-out cut the episodes with a violation from 31.7\% to 7.9\%; a gate on the call's step alone cut them to 17.9\% (relative reductions 0.75 and 0.44; difference \GLbPmP). Success did not change with the pre-call gate (18.7\%); the position gate raised it (27.8\%). The offline share of violating calls that the pre-call gate holds, fixed before the analysis, predicted its own effect (0.75 vs 0.75) but missed the position gate's (0.17 vs 0.44). A first design, at unequal review rates, did not separate the two gates, and on Gemma-4, whose pre-call read-out ranks weakly, a pre-call gate did not prevent. In the CAPE-Bench live tests, CAPE's checker stands in for the human reviewer.

\textbf{Prevention with no reviewer.} In the tests above, a held call went to a reviewer. Here nothing judges in the loop: when the agent's own read-out flags a call, the call is held once and the agent is told to re-check its policy and the facts; an identical re-proposal runs. On Claude Haiku 5.5's unapproved production restarts (41 scenarios, 492 fresh units), the episodes with a violation fell from \GSgNone\ to \GSgSelf\ (relative reduction \GSgRed), against \GSgRand\ for random holds (difference \GSgDiff). Random holds came at a lower rate (holds per call \GSgHold); scaled linearly to the same rate, their reduction would be about 0.35, so the read-out's gain over random holding is about 0.12. Success rose by \GSgSucc, as it did with random holds. The forecast can also act before the call: when the agent's sampled policy forecasts a violation, a short policy reminder is added before it acts, with nothing held. On 492 fresh units, violating episodes fell from \GFsNone\ to \GFsFc\ (relative reduction \GFsRed) and success rose from 13\% to 40\%. The reminder itself does most of this: given at random turns, more often (0.39 vs 0.31 per turn), it reached \GFsRand.
