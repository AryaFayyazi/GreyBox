\section{Reconstructing the hidden geometry from answers}
\label{sec:geom-results}
We first test the reconstruction where the truth is visible: on open models, whose hidden states we store.
\textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (1{,}440 held out for confirmation) and 2{,}000 fresh contexts that add four kinds (news, programming, jailbreaks, prompt injections); 88 questions; eight open chat models from seven families. Ground truth: each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at a layer chosen on development data (\cref{app:details}).


\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/geometry_main.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{What yes/no answers reveal of a hidden state.} (a) Agreement of each open model's answer geometry with its own hidden geometry, exact and from 8 sampled answers, against the geometry of the input text (Spearman over all pairs of 1{,}440 held-out contexts; nothing fit). (b) Answers of model A against the hidden geometry of model B on 1{,}000 fresh contexts: the own-model diagonal (outlined) is not special.}
\label{fig:geometry}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Answers beat the input and the replies.} On every model, the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input does (gains \GOneGain; every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; \cref{fig:geometry}a, \cref{tab:geometry}). The model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, do little better than the input (\GFiveRep). Agreement rises with the number of questions (from 8 to 88: \GOneMore), and 8 sampled answers per question keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts of four new kinds, answers beat the input on 6 of 8 models. Raw $P(\textsc{yes})$ ranks about as well as the exact angles; what the angles add is a known noise, which the read-out's label-free ceiling uses (\cref{sec:monitor}).

\textbf{The geometry is shared across models.} A model's answers match another model's hidden geometry exactly as well as its own. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the mean own-model excess is \GSixExcess, inside the equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ that we fixed in advance (\cref{fig:geometry}b); the hidden geometries of different models also agree with each other (\GSixHH). So what the answers reveal is the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}. It also predicts the next section's result at the call: the shared part is what any model's answers can read.

\textbf{A closed model reveals the same shared geometry.} Claude Haiku 5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of its answers give the same geometry (Spearman \GTwoSplit; bound fixed in advance: 0.7), which carries \GTwoText\ beyond the input embedding. Its answers agree with open models' hidden geometries at \GTwoOpen.

\textbf{Its own hidden geometry, by triangulation.} Its hidden states are not visible. If, as on open models, no part of its answers is specific to its own state, triangulation (\cref{prop:triang}) estimates its own agreement at 0.49 (bootstrap interval [0.44, 0.55]; [0.32, 0.53] over the range of open models' loadings). Leaving each open model out as if closed, the same estimate predicts the true agreement to within \GVtMae\ on average (\GVtInside\ of 8 inside the predicted interval); a model-blind estimate, the mean of the other open models, does as well (0.053), but it gives every closed model the same value, while triangulation uses the closed model's own answers.

\section{Monitoring, forecasting and preventing violations}
\label{sec:results}
This section tests monitoring, forecasting and prevention: at the call, a fixed read-out monitors as well as a probe and carries to new benchmarks and closed agents (\cref{sec:monitor,sec:carry}); before the call, the forecast is the agent's own policy (\cref{sec:forecast}); and gates on these reads prevent violations by a predictable amount (\cref{sec:prevent-live}).

\textbf{Agents and benchmarks.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised; its checker labels each action. Open agents, whose decision states we store: Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B, Muse-Glimmer-30B. Closed agents, through their public APIs with reasoning off: Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6-luna (six templates). External benchmarks: AgentDojo and Sierra's $\tau^2$-bench airline.

\subsection{Monitoring at the call}
\label{sec:monitor}
\textbf{The read-out.} At each proposed call, the agent answers 8 yes/no questions about the call through its own API, 4 times each (\cref{app:questions}). The fixed read-out (\cref{eq:readout}; questions fixed on development data, no labels) scores the answers.

\textbf{The reconstructed geometry alone flags violations.} A call's Fisher--Rao distance from the agent's typical state, with no label and no question sign, ranks the violating calls among the drawn calls of both closed agents: \GGeoClaude\ on Claude Haiku 5.5 (1{,}765 calls) and \GGeoGpt\ on GPT-6-luna (832 calls). On the open agents' at-call answers it reaches 0.76--0.98 (exploratory). The signed read-out below is one projection of this geometry; it adds the direction of each question (0.89 and 0.99 on the same calls).

\textbf{It ranks violations about as well as a probe on the hidden states.} On held-out calls, the within-template AUROC is \GFtFixed\ (\cref{tab:g15}). A probe trained on the agent's true decision states, a comparison that needs the weights and labels, scores \GFtTrainedWB; the mean difference is \GFtTtwo. The read-out beats the agent's single self-audit answer by \GFtTone, and a Gemma-4 reasoning judge on the open agents by \GTwJone; on Claude Haiku 5.5, answered with reasoning off, the judge is better (\cref{tab:g15}). It does not need the full decision state: the geometry of the 8 answers agrees with that state at only 0.01--0.16, and on open agents about 20\% [13, 27] of its ranking power runs through the hidden violation direction. The rest comes from the transcript, which every reader sees.

\textbf{With no label, the read-out tells where it works.} This is where the geometry's coordinates pay off: their sampling noise is known (\cref{thm:geometry}), so the read-out's variance beyond that noise, its SNR, needs no label (\cref{prop:snrceil}). Over 29 cells (agent, benchmark and stratum), the SNR predicts the AUROC (Spearman 0.71): cells with SNR $>0$ score 0.96 on average, cells with SNR $\le0$ score 0.68 (the nominal noise is conservative, so a low SNR flags a setting without ruling it out). Claude Haiku 5.5 has the lowest SNR: its four answers agree on 96--97\% of questions.

\textbf{At the call, another answerer does as well, as the theory implies.} The transcript and the call decide the violation (\cref{cor:action}), and the answers read a geometry that models share. So another model that answers the same questions about the same call should match the agent. It does on open agents (own $-$ other +0.012 [$-$0.018, 0.040]), and it does better on Claude Haiku 5.5 ($-$0.09 on restarts; $-$0.28 on $\tau^2$ with reasoning on). Reasoning helps at the call: Claude Haiku 5.5 reviewing its own $\tau^2$ writes with reasoning on reaches 0.918 (180 writes), against 0.699 for our read-out on the same writes, which runs with reasoning off for cost. Monitoring at the call thus works through the agent's own API, but it does not need the agent; before the call, the forecast is the agent's policy (\cref{sec:forecast}).

\subsection{Carrying to new benchmarks and closed agents}
\label{sec:carry}
\textbf{Prompt injection and GPT-6-luna.} On AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, where the violation is a call that carries out an instruction hidden in a tool output, 8 questions written from that policy before any answer existed rank the open agents' injected calls within suite at \GSvGemma\ (Gemma-4) and \GSvGpt\ (gpt-oss-20b). Through its own API, GPT-6-luna's answers rank its CAPE-Bench violations within template and tool at \GGlAt\ (single answer 0.795).

\textbf{Claude Haiku 5.5 on CAPE-Bench.} The read-out ranks its unapproved restarts at \GFtClosedAFixed, where its single self-audit answer scores 0.32; pooled over all its actions it scores 0.475, because a fact it never checked decides its mailing-list sends.

\textbf{Claude Haiku 5.5 on $\tau^2$-bench.} With 8 questions written from the airline policy before any answer existed, the within-tool AUROC on its writes is \GEeRone\ (\GEeN; single answer \GEeSingle); in 5 of the 7 tasks it is 0.77--1.00 (exploratory), and in the other two the agent misread the booking.

\subsection{Forecasting before the call}
\label{sec:forecast}
\textbf{The pre-call state.} Before each step's call, the agent answers 8 questions about its next action. The call does not exist yet, so no reader of the call's text can help, and comparing calls at the same step removes the call's position.

\textbf{GPT-6-luna's state forecasts the coming violation.} On 252 fresh episodes (1{,}047 calls, 68 violations), its pre-call answers ranked the coming violation at 0.84 within template and tool, where position alone scores 0.78, and at 0.92 among calls at the same position (9 strata, 27 violations). A second fresh set gave \GFfGptPos\ [0.673, 0.939] (per-agent value of a pooled test).

\textbf{Two ways to read the policy.} \Cref{prop:selfforecast} says the forecast is the agent's own policy. It can be read by asking (the self-report) or by sampling (\cref{cor:rollout}): draw the agent's next action 8 times in side branches and read each drawn call with its own answers. A text monitor, by contrast, guesses the policy from the transcript; it loses as far as its guess differs from the policy.

\textbf{When text suffices: GPT-6-luna.} Its words track its actions. On 504 new episodes (1{,}134 calls), among calls at the same position, its self-report ranks the coming violation at 0.84, GPT-6-luna itself reasoning over the transcript at \GGjJudge, Gemma-4 answering in its seat as well as GPT-6-luna does, and its own sampled actions at \GGjOwn\ (own $-$ text monitor \GGjDiff). Its draws are calibrated to its actual actions (+0.015 [0.008, 0.023]): its loop allows one tool call per turn, so our replay is exact. An earlier test had found its own answers better than another model's (+0.111 [0.041, 0.185]); on these new episodes they were not.

\textbf{When text does not suffice: Claude Haiku 5.5.} Its words do not track its actions, as CAPE found for open agents. On 492 new episodes (\GRoN), its self-report ranks the coming violation among calls at the same position at \GRoReport, while its own sampled actions reach \GRoOwn\ (difference \GRoOwnMinusReport; \cref{fig:saysdoes}). On a second set of 492 episodes with exact contexts, Claude Haiku 5.5 itself reasoning over the full transcript scores \GJdJudge, and its sampled actions \GJdOwn\ (difference \GJdDiff); GPT-6-luna reasoning over the same transcripts reaches \GXjJudge\ (own $-$ it \GXjDiff). Gemma-4 sampling in its seat scores \GRoOther\ at the same position; pooled over positions its draws score \GRoOtherTT\ because they track the step at which Claude violates, so that registered comparison failed. Per decision, the policy read costs \$0.002 and 9\,s, the text monitor \$0.0004 and 3.5\,s (median over 100 decisions). On $\tau^2$-bench airline the policy forecast did not separate from chance (0.605 [0.444, 0.835], 7 tasks): before a write, Claude's draws mostly repeat the write.

\textbf{The geometry alone forecasts.} Placing the agent's 8 sampled next actions in the Fisher--Rao coordinates and averaging their distance from its typical state gives a forecast with no label and no question sign: \GGfClaude\ on Claude Haiku 5.5 and \GGfGpt\ on GPT-6-luna, among calls at the same position. For Claude it matches the signed policy forecast and far exceeds both text monitors.

\textbf{Telling the two cases apart, without labels} (exploratory). Among calls at the same position, the rank agreement between what the agent says about its next action and what its sampled policy forecasts is $-0.01$ for Claude Haiku 5.5 and $+0.25$ for GPT-6-luna.

\textbf{Does the theory predict a reader's loss in size?} For Gemma-4 in Claude's seat, the policies differ (gap \GPgGap), but the predicted loss (0.102) exceeded the observed (0.056): Claude's draws were miscalibrated (0.17 vs 0.21) because our replay dropped refused extra tool calls. Tests with exact contexts and with four readers on GPT-6-luna are preregistered and running (\cref{app:prereg}).



\subsection{Preventing violations}
\label{sec:prevent-live}
\textbf{Self-policing.} Before a non-read call runs, a side branch asks the agent whether the call complies with its policy, and a flagged call goes to review. At one state, a gate prevents the share $\tau$ of violating calls that the agent flags. Over many states, it prevents the mean of $\tau$, weighted by where the violations occur (\cref{prop:prevention}).


\textbf{Live results.} On unseen scenarios (\cref{tab:selfpolice}), Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes and reached more goals. Claude Haiku 5.5, asked to check its policy rule by rule, prevented 69\% of the violating episodes that its transcript decides (2.8\% of its episodes; 31\% over all scenarios), at no cost in goals. Against prompt injection on AgentDojo, the gate cut attack success by 84\% and 50\%, at no cost in utility.

\textbf{The effect is predictable.} In a preregistered test, offline flag rates, weighted by where each agent violates, predicted the live reduction of five agents within the registered 0.15 (reductions from 0.01 to 0.63; mean absolute error 0.035; \cref{fig:law}). An earlier form, with flag rates from another mix of tasks, failed, and on AgentDojo the law missed Gemma-4 by 0.16.

\textbf{A gate on the pre-call state.} Live, on fresh GPT-6-luna episodes, we compared two gates at an equal review rate (about 1.5 reviews per episode). A gate on the pre-call read-out cut the episodes with a violation from 31.7\% to 7.9\%; a gate on the call's step alone cut them to 17.9\% (relative reductions 0.75 and 0.44; difference \GLbPmP). Success did not change with the pre-call gate (18.7\%); the position gate raised it (27.8\%). The offline share of violating calls that the pre-call gate holds, fixed before the analysis, predicted its own effect (0.75 vs 0.75) but missed the position gate's (0.17 vs 0.44). A first design, at unequal review rates, did not separate the two gates, and on Gemma-4, whose pre-call read-out ranks weakly, a pre-call gate did not prevent. In the CAPE-Bench live tests, CAPE's checker stands in for the human reviewer.

\textbf{Prevention with no reviewer.} In the tests above, a held call went to a reviewer. Here nothing judges in the loop: when the agent's own read-out flags a call, the call is held once and the agent is told to re-check its policy and the facts; an identical re-proposal runs. On Claude Haiku 5.5's unapproved production restarts (41 scenarios, 492 fresh units), the episodes with a violation fell from \GSgNone\ to \GSgSelf\ (relative reduction \GSgRed), against \GSgRand\ for random holds (difference \GSgDiff). Random holds came at a lower rate (holds per call \GSgHold); scaled linearly to the same rate, their reduction would be about 0.35, so the read-out's gain over random holding is about 0.12. Success rose by \GSgSucc, as it did with random holds. On GPT-6-luna, over six templates (252 units per arm), the same reviewer-free hold cut violating episodes from \GSgGNone\ to \GSgGSelf\ (relative reduction \GSgGRed), against \GSgGRand\ for random holds, which held more calls (difference \GSgGDiff); success changed by \GSgGSucc, narrowly missing its registered bar of $-0.05$. Where the transcript decides the violation, prevention was near total (45\% to 0\% of episodes on one template); where an unchecked fact decides it, nothing changed, as \cref{cor:action} predicts. The forecast can also act before the call: when the agent's sampled policy forecasts a violation, a short policy reminder is added before it acts, with nothing held. On 492 fresh units, violating episodes fell from \GFsNone\ to \GFsFc\ (relative reduction \GFsRed) and success rose from 13\% to 40\%. The reminder itself does most of this: given at random turns, more often (0.39 vs 0.31 per turn), it reached \GFsRand.
