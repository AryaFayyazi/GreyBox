\section{Estimating the hidden geometry through the API}
\label{sec:geom-results}
We first test the reconstruction where the truth is visible: on open models, whose hidden states we store.
\textbf{Data.} 4{,}800 contexts of six kinds (1{,}440 held out) and 1{,}000 fresh ones with four new kinds; 88 questions; eight open models from seven families (\cref{app:details}). Ground truth: each model's residual stream at the last prompt token, at a layer chosen on development data (\cref{app:details}).

(b) Answers of model A against the hidden geometry of model B on 1{,}000 fresh contexts: the own-model diagonal (outlined) is not special.}

\textbf{Answers beat the input and the replies.} On every model, the answer geometry matches the hidden geometry better than a sentence embedding of the input does (gains \GOneGain; every lower bound at least \GOneGainLow; \cref{fig:geometry}a, \cref{tab:geometry}). The model's own replies, the other output that an API gives, do little better than the input (\GFiveRep). Agreement rises with more questions (\GOneMore); 8 sampled answers per question keep \GOneKeep\ of the exact agreement. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts (400 of them of four new kinds), answers beat the input on 6 of 8 models. Against the strongest of five text baselines (three sentence embedders, on the input or the replies), answers win on 7 of 8 models on held-out contexts (gpt-oss ties) and on 6 of 8 on fresh ones (Gemma-3 and gpt-oss lose by 0.10 and 0.15; exploratory). Raw $P(\textsc{yes})$ ranks about as well; the angles add a known noise, which the label-free ceiling uses (\cref{sec:monitor}).

\textbf{The geometry is shared across models.} A model's answers match another model's hidden geometry exactly as well as its own. On 1{,}000 fresh contexts, the mean own-model excess is \GSixExcess, inside the equivalence band of $\pm0.02$ that we fixed in advance (\cref{fig:geometry}b). So what the answers reveal is the geometry that models share, in line with converging representations \citep{huh2024platonic,picozzi2026information}. It also predicts the next section's result at the call: the shared part is what any model's answers can read.

\textbf{Which channel reveals what.} On 1{,}000 further fresh contexts (preregistered), sampled yes/no answers carry no own-model excess (+0.001), while at the last layer the model-private part grows with the output channel (top-1 +0.056, top-20 +0.115, log-probabilities +0.221 [0.211, 0.230]); log-probabilities pick out the model in 7 of 8 cases, answers in 1. Yes/no answers through a closed API therefore expose the shared geometry of the state (sampled free text can still identify a model \citep{pasquini2024llmmap,gao2024model}), and the agent's private part only through what it does (\cref{sec:forecast}).

\textbf{A short battery estimates a slice of the decision state.} By \cref{thm:geometry}(c), $m$ questions estimate the hidden geometry in the metric they pull back, a slice of rank at most $m$, not the full state. At agents' proposed calls, 8 policy questions agree with the full decision-state geometry at only 0.01--0.16; a linear map from the decision state to the answers, fit across scenarios with no label, gives the slice they pull back, and the answer geometry agrees with those predictions at 0.60, 0.36, 0.38 and 0.58 on the four open agents, against 0.02--0.10 for random rank-8 maps of the state (all 4 lower bounds above both; secondary). 32 broader questions recover more of the full state (+0.12 [0.08, 0.16], still 0.18) but their geometry does not flag violations (0.50; policy battery 0.58--0.96).

\textbf{A closed model reveals the shared geometry, on familiar kinds of context.} Claude Haiku 5.5 answered 24 questions, 8 times each, on 192 held-out contexts. Two independent halves of its answers give the same geometry (Spearman \GTwoSplit; bar 0.7). Its answers agree with open models' hidden geometries at \GTwoOpen. On 400 fresh contexts that include new kinds (news, code, jailbreaks, injections), a preregistered test failed: its 24 answers agreed with the open models' hidden geometries at only 0.10--0.48, and its own replies, embedded, scored higher on 7 of 8 models (significantly on 5). A closed model's general geometry is estimated well only on contexts like those its questions were written for; the agent results below use batteries written for the agent's task. With no own-model excess, triangulation (\cref{prop:triang}) estimates Claude's agreement with its own hidden geometry at 0.49 [0.44, 0.55] (leave-one-out error \GVtMae; 0.114 on fresh contexts).

\section{Monitoring, forecasting and preventing violations}
\label{sec:results}
\textbf{Agents and benchmarks.} CAPE-Bench \citep{fayyazi2026cape} gives matched tool-use tasks in which one fact decides whether an action is authorised; its checker labels each action. Open agents (decision states stored): Gemma-4 (26B), gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B, Muse-Glimmer-30B. Closed agents, through their APIs, reasoning off: Claude Haiku 5.5, GPT-6-luna. External benchmarks: AgentDojo and Sierra's $\tau^2$-bench airline.

\subsection{Monitoring at the call}
\label{sec:monitor}
\textbf{The read-out.} At each proposed call, the agent answers 8 yes/no questions about it through its own API, 4 times each (\cref{app:questions}), scored by the fixed read-out (\cref{eq:readout}; no labels).

\textbf{It ranks violations about as well as a probe on the hidden states.} On held-out calls, the within-template AUROC is \GFtFixed\ (\cref{tab:g15}). A probe trained on true decision states (it needs weights and labels) scores \GFtTrainedWB; the mean difference is \GFtTtwo. The read-out beats the agent's single self-audit answer by \GFtTone. On the open agents, it also beats a Gemma-4 judge that reasons, by \GTwJone. On Claude Haiku 5.5, whose answers we take with reasoning off, the judge is better (\cref{tab:g15}). The read-out reads the slice of the state its questions pull back (\cref{sec:geom-results}), not the full state: on open agents about 20\% [13, 27] of its ranking power runs through the hidden violation direction, the rest through the transcript, which any monitor can see.

\textbf{With no label, the read-out's SNR predicts where it works.} This is where the geometry's coordinates pay off: their sampling noise is known (\cref{thm:geometry}), so the read-out's variance beyond that noise, its SNR, needs no label (\cref{prop:snrceil}). Over 29 cells (agent, benchmark and stratum), the SNR predicts the AUROC (Spearman 0.71): cells with SNR $>0$ score 0.96 on average, cells with SNR $\le0$ score 0.68 (the nominal noise is conservative, so a low SNR flags a setting without ruling it out).

\textbf{At the call, another answerer does as well, as the theory implies.} The transcript decides the violation (\cref{cor:action}) and the answers read a geometry that models share, so another model answering about the same call should match the agent: on open agents it does (own $-$ other +0.012 [$-$0.018, 0.040]), and on Claude Haiku 5.5 it does better ($-$0.09 on restarts; $-$0.28 on $\tau^2$ with reasoning on). Reasoning may help: with it on, Claude reviewing its own $\tau^2$ writes reaches 0.918, against 0.699 for our read-out (180 writes; not significant).

\textbf{Other benchmarks and closed agents.}\label{sec:carry} On AgentDojo \citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, 8 questions written from the injection policy before any answer existed rank the open agents' injected calls at \GSvGemma\ and \GSvGpt. GPT-6-luna's answers rank its violations at \GGlAt. Claude Haiku 5.5's rank its unapproved restarts at \GFtClosedAFixed, but pooled score 0.475: a fact it never checked decides its mailing-list sends; on $\tau^2$-bench, \GEeRone.

\subsection{Forecasting before the call}
\label{sec:forecast}
\input{tab_forecast}
\textbf{The pre-call state.} Before each call, the agent answers 8 questions about its next action (its self-report); \cref{tab:forecast} compares every forecaster on the same calls, at the same step.

\textbf{When text suffices: GPT-6-luna.} Its words track its actions: its self-report (0.84) and a text monitor (0.78) forecast as well as its sampled actions (0.73), and so does Gemma-4 sampling from its contexts (0.78; descriptive); read after the data, as the registered opposite expectation failed.

\textbf{When text does not suffice: Claude Haiku 5.5.} Claude Haiku 5.5 rarely violates (2 of 24 CAPE-Bench templates; 1.5--1.6\% on AgentDojo and $\tau^2$), and on one template it violates nearly always; its tests use the other, unapproved production restarts (41 scenarios), with exact contexts.

Among calls at the same step, its self-report and both reasoning text monitors are near chance or weak; only its sampled next actions forecast which call violates (\cref{tab:forecast}; own $-$ self-report \GSeDiff; own $-$ GPT-6-luna's judge \GXjDiff). Its words are nearly constant: before every violating call, each answer to whether it would comply said yes, and before 93\% (94\% fresh) of them none of its 32 answers admitted a violation; its rare admissions came before safe calls (descriptive). Gemma-4 sampling from Claude's contexts does not (0.54 at the same step).

Across steps, Gemma-4's draws tie Claude's own (0.82 vs 0.80; exploratory; the registered test on rebuilt contexts found Gemma-4's draws better, $-$0.14 [$-$0.20, $-$0.09]), and Claude's draws alone (0.80) score below the step alone (0.82): across steps, other readers track \emph{when} Claude violates; within a step, only its own policy tells \emph{which} call (+0.35 [0.28, 0.42]; exploratory). The sampled actions still add to the best text monitor across steps (0.90 to 0.96; on GPT-6-luna they do not, 0.78 to 0.73, descriptive). 

\textbf{The registered results replicated.} On 492 fresh episodes (\GRpN\ calls), in a test registered with its Holm correction before any episode existed, all 8 endpoints passed: own draws $-$ self-report \GRpOne\ (primary), $-$ the two text monitors \GRpTwo\ and \GRpThree, lift across steps \GRpFour\ (the lift was first found on existing data, secondary). The policy read costs \$0.002 per decision (median 9\,s). On $\tau^2$-bench the forecast was not above chance (0.61 [0.44, 0.84]).

\textbf{Sampling, not the reader, matters.} GPT-6-luna reading Claude's sampled calls beats reading the transcript (\GOrDone; fresh \GRpSeven), and Claude's own reads (equivalence failed).

\textbf{The geometry alone forecasts.} With no label and no question sign, the mean distance of the agent's sampled next actions from its typical state in the estimated geometry forecasts its coming violation: 0.89 on Claude Haiku 5.5 (0.87 on fresh episodes, registered before they existed), weakly 0.64 on GPT-6-luna, and on average 0.75 [0.68, 0.81] over four open agents across their task families (Gemma-4 0.77, gpt-oss-20b 0.75, Qwen3.8 0.91, Muse-Glimmer 0.56 [0.44, 0.68]; secondary: on existing draws).

\textbf{What the weights add.} A probe on CAPE's stored decision state forecasts better: the registered comparison failed, the probe leading by 0.16 [0.04, 0.28] (Gemma-4 0.97 vs 0.78; gpt-oss-20b 0.82 vs 0.70). That state is read after the agent's own hidden reasoning for the turn. On Gemma-4, a probe at the start of the turn, the moment a closed API forecasts from, scores 0.80 on 217 units, against 0.78 for the API geometry and 0.80 for the signed API forecast; turn-start minus after-reasoning probe is $-$0.20 [$-$0.31, $-$0.10]. So most of the lead may come from the reasoning, but the registered test at turn start ($-$0.02 [$-$0.16, 0.12], bar $-$0.05) was too small to pass; a powered test is under way.

\textbf{The reconstructed geometry alone flags violations.} Before each step, both closed agents drew calls in side branches, and CAPE's checker labelled them. With no label and no question sign, a drawn call's Fisher--Rao distance from the agent's typical state ranks the violating ones: \GGeoClaude\ on Claude Haiku 5.5 (1{,}765 calls, restarts only, so within tool) and \GGeoGpt\ on GPT-6-luna (832 calls; the interval is wide; secondary). The signed read-out of \cref{sec:monitor} is one projection of this geometry; it adds the direction of each question and scores higher (0.89 and 0.99 on the same calls). On the fresh episodes, the geometric monitor scored 0.81.

\textbf{The theory predicts the loss of any forecaster that does not sample.} By \cref{prop:selfforecast}, a forecast $q$ from the context alone loses $\E(f-q)^2$ in Brier score against the agent's policy $f$; the agent's draws, checked by CAPE's checker, estimate this without a label on the executed call (\cref{cor:rollout}). Registered before any episode existed, the predicted loss of another model's draws matched the observed on both closed agents (a first Claude test, with contexts rebuilt from logs, failed: $-$0.046): 0.122 vs 0.126 on Claude Haiku 5.5 (difference \mbox{+0.004} [$-$0.003, 0.010]) and 0.018 vs 0.020 on GPT-6-luna (+0.002 [$-$0.004, 0.008]; bar $\pm0.02$). On the fresh episodes they matched again (\GRpSixA\ and \GRpSixB); so did three text monitors' (secondary). The prediction needs checked draws: with GPT-6-luna's judgments in place of the checker it failed, even after recalibration. A registered Brier separation of the two regimes failed (poor calibration).

\subsection{Preventing violations}
\label{sec:prevent-live}
\textbf{Self-policing.} A side branch asks the agent whether a non-read call complies with its policy; a flagged call goes to review, and a gate prevents the flagged share of violating calls (\cref{prop:prevention}).

\textbf{Live results} (checker as reviewer; \cref{tab:selfpolice}). Gemma-4 prevented 82\% of its violating episodes, Claude Haiku 5.5 69\% of those its transcript decides (31\% over all), and on AgentDojo attack success fell by 84\% and 50\%, with no loss in goals or utility.

\textbf{The effect is predictable.} Offline flag rates, weighted by where each agent violates, predicted five agents' live reductions within 0.15 (mean error 0.035; \cref{fig:law}); an earlier form failed, and it missed AgentDojo Gemma-4 by 0.16.

\textbf{A gate on the pre-call state.} On fresh GPT-6-luna episodes, at an equal review rate (checker as reviewer), a gate on the pre-call read-out cut violating episodes from 31.7\% to 7.9\%, a gate on the step to 17.9\% (\GLbPmP; success 18.7\% vs 27.8\% with the step gate). The law predicted the first exactly (0.75) but under-predicted the step gate (0.17 vs 0.44); an earlier design at unequal rates did not separate the gates, and on Gemma-4 a pre-call gate did not prevent.

\textbf{Prevention with no reviewer.} A flagged call is held once and the agent told to re-check; an identical re-proposal runs. Holding the calls Claude's signed read-out flags cut violating episodes from 67.9\% to 35.2\%, against 42.9\% for random holds at a matched dose (+0.114 [0.035, 0.200]). 

\textbf{The geometry alone gates, beyond random holds.} A gate on the distance from the agent's typical state (cut set on unlabelled calls; no label, no question sign) cut Claude's violating episodes from 65.9\% to 44.9\% on 492 fresh units, but at a matched dose random holds did about as well (48.2\%; +0.033 [$-$0.035, 0.100], failed). Claude's answers are nearly unanimous, so distances tie: half of the violating calls sat at the cut, where that gate held by a coin (exploratory). Registered with the tie removed (hold at or above the cut), the gate left 20.1\% of episodes violating against 32.3\% for random holds at the same dose (0.32 vs 0.30 holds per call; +0.122 [0.071, 0.177]). Holds stack on a policy reminder: 21.1\% with the reminder, 10.8\% with both (0.49 [0.34, 0.62]).

On GPT-6-luna (six templates) it cut them from \GSgGNone\ to \GSgGSelf, against \GSgGRand\ for random holds (\GSgGDiff; success \GSgGSucc, lower bound just below the registered $-0.05$); where an unchecked fact decides the violation, nothing changed (\cref{cor:action}). Forecast-timed reminders did no better than random (\GFsFc\ vs \GFsRand).
