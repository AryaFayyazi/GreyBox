This appendix collects the limits of every claim and every test that failed or could not be done. The main text reports
only results that met their preregistered criterion; the complete record, with each test's outcome as it was registered,

\subsection{Scope of the claims}
\textbf{The observable geometry.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{What each API channel reveals (G7), in full.}\label{app:channels} Is the sharing a property of outputs in general, or of answers? For each channel an API can return, simulated with exact open-model probabilities on 1{,}000 further fresh contexts, we measured the own-model excess against the last layer (preregistered; \cref{fig:channels}a). It rises with the channel: yes/no answers \GSevLAns; the top 1, 5 and 20 next tokens \GSevLTopOne, \GSevLTopFive\ and \GSevLTopTwenty; the full next-token law \GSevLFull; and centred log-probability vectors \GSevLLogprob, which pick out a model's own final-layer geometry among the eight for \GSevBestLogprob\ models (answers: \GSevBestAns). The last is close to guaranteed, since log-probabilities are a linear image of the final state (\cref{thm:exact}). So answers reveal what models share, while next-token probabilities leak part of the model itself (\GSevOut); this qualifies the sharing that \citet{picozzi2026information} report for next-token laws.
\item \textbf{Log-probabilities at the chosen layer.} Against the layer chosen on development data (not the last), the log-probability excess is \GSevCLogprob\ and the own model is the best match for \GSevCBestLogprob.
\item \textbf{The answers do not reveal a model-specific geometry.} A model's answers match other models' hidden geometries as well as its own (own-model excess $+0.002$ on fresh contexts; preregistered), in every category of question we tried (exploratory). Whatever part of a model's hidden geometry is its own is invisible in its answers to our battery; only full log-probabilities recover the model's own final geometry. A different battery, or behaviour of another kind \citep{schiekiera2026associations}, might reveal more.
\item \textbf{Scale, and a small pooled excess.} In an exploratory check with four larger models on the same fresh contexts (Qwen3 14B and 32B, Gemma-4 26B, Llama-3.3 70B), the pooled own-model excess over 12 models is \ScaleAll, small but above zero at 75\% depth, and \ScaleLlama\ for Llama-3.3 70B. In the Qwen3 series it rises a little with size (\ScaleQwen), which the Llama pair (8B, 70B) does not show. Individual models deviate from the average in both directions (\GSixPerModel). Frontier closed models are larger still; we cannot test them.
\item \textbf{The geometry does not choose the reader.} On development data of three open agents, a reader's label-free geometric agreement with an agent's own decision-state geometry did not predict how well a monitor built on that reader transfers to the agent, beyond separating LLM readers from sentence encoders: across 24 reader--agent pairs the Spearman correlation was $+0.03$, and among five Qwen3 readers it vanished or reversed. The monitor's validity rests on \cref{cor:action}, not on the geometry.
\item \textbf{Agreement, not identity.} The answer geometry agrees with the hidden geometry at Spearman \GOneAns, not 1. \Cref{thm:geometry}(c) relates them through the pulled-back Fisher metric, so equality is not expected; the hidden geometry also holds directions that no question reads (surface form). The ground truth is one layer, at the last prompt token.
\item \textbf{Within one kind of context the agreement is weaker.} Over each of the six kinds separately (240 contexts), the median $\rho$ with the hidden geometry is \WKAns\ for answers and \WKText\ for text, and the answers are ahead in \WKCells\ model--kind cells; for some models the text is ahead on benign requests or maths problems. Pooled over all same-kind pairs, the text is ahead for 7 of 8 models (Mistral is the exception), although that statistic mixes the distance scales of different kinds (exploratory; \texttt{results/t1/within\_kind\_exploratory.json}).
\item \textbf{The battery matters.} Agreement rises with the number of questions, and for four of eight models it still rises from 64 to 88; eight questions are not enough, and a larger battery may reveal more. Our battery asks about the message; another battery measures another part of the state.
\item \textbf{For a closed model we show stability, not truth.} Claude's geometry is the same in two halves of its samples and differs from the text geometry; its relation to Claude's hidden states cannot be tested.
\item \textbf{Replies are the more sensitive output for a change of state} (below), and on the fresh contexts of G6 one model's replies (Gemma) tracked its hidden geometry better than its answers (0.43 vs 0.34). Agreement on those fresh contexts was lower than on the first ones for every model (answers \GSixAns).
\item \textbf{Exact recovery needs full log-probabilities} and $d(d+1)/2$ contexts ($5\times10^5$ for $d=1{,}024$; $8\times10^6$ for $d=4{,}096$). Closed APIs return at most 20 log-probabilities. It recovers the final normalised state only, and \citet{carlini2024stealing,finlayson2024logits} use the same structure.
\item \textbf{One reply embedder} (MiniLM, as for the text); a stronger embedder could raise the reply geometry.
\item \textbf{A correction of our theory (after the lock).} The locked Theorem 5 stated the error of the variance-subtraction rule wrongly; Correction 1 and the exact cross-fit replace it (\cref{app:proofs}). The preregistered endpoints used the locked rule and are reported as registered.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Monitoring agents.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Replication is tested only where the true state is known} (four open agents). For closed agents we test
  prediction and prevention with a probe on the reader's state; the replica itself, mapped into Gemma-4's coordinates,
  scores lower on the closed agents (AUROC 0.959 and 0.976; exploratory). Replication is partial: 46--66\% of the
  variance of the main components, 51--54\% of each full layer on Gemma-4 and 30--46\% on the other three agents.
\item \textbf{The replica's gain is shrinkage, not the reader alone.} The true-state probe also scores higher on a TF-IDF replica than on the reader replica for the three new agents (within template 0.934, 0.977, 0.971 vs 0.924, 0.972, 0.969; exploratory): any conditional-mean estimate removes label-free variance.
\item \textbf{The decisive information is largely in the tokens.} A probe on the true state also works on a TF-IDF replica
  (0.983), and TF-IDF with the same labels equals the reader on the training benchmark (within-template AUROC 0.997 vs 0.991 on Claude; pooled, 0.975 vs 0.989; the
  preregistered test that the reader beats it there failed). The reader's advantage is robustness to shift.
\item \textbf{Most agent tests use one benchmark}, CAPE-Bench, whose tasks are synthetic. The headline closed-agent
  results (94\% and 98\% caught; live prevention) are on the benchmark the monitor was trained on.
\item \textbf{A monitored agent and an adaptive attacker, in full.} Told in its system prompt that a safety monitor reviews every tool call, Claude violated as often as before, and the monitor caught 97.1\% of its violations at the frozen cut (83.6\% when not told; $+0.136$ [0.050, 0.225]; preregistered). The notice moved all scores up, so the frozen cut escalated 15.2\% of actions instead of 5\%, within the Kolmogorov bound of \cref{prop:shift} ($K=0.24$); a cut set again on the told agent's unlabelled traffic escalated 4.7\% live and still removed every executed violation (\cref{tab:live}). An attacker who can query the score and rewrites only the free text of a violating call lowered the share caught from 96\% to 29\%; two preregistered defenses failed (51\% and 60\%), close to the ceiling of \cref{prop:ceiling} (\cref{app:limits}). The score must stay hidden from the agent.
\item \textbf{Self-policing is verification, not self-knowledge.} In a cross-audit on CAPE-Bench development data, gpt-oss judged Gemma-4's calls (AUROC 0.878) better than its own (0.741), and Gemma-4 judged gpt-oss's at 0.780; the self $\times$ agent interaction is $+0.03$. An agent polices itself well when it is a competent judge of the policy and its violations are slips.
\item \textbf{The training-free read-out (G15) has four limits.} (i) Its 8 questions were written from CAPE-Bench's kinds of violation; another policy needs its own questions, written before any data (G17 and G18 do this for AgentDojo and $\tau^2$-bench). (ii) Claude's restart template is easy for a reader of the call's text (a trained reader scores 1.000; its allowed calls are mostly help and status commands), so T3 shows the read-out nearly reaching a text ceiling, not solving a hard case. (iii) It cannot flag a violation decided by a fact the agent never checked: on Claude, only 4.8\% of the mailing-list sends score above the median allowed call, so its pooled AUROC on Claude is 0.475 although restarts score 0.909. (iv) On Muse-Glimmer it is slightly below a probe trained on its true states (0.929 vs 0.941), and a probe tuned afterwards (exploratory: principal components and regularisation chosen by cross-validation on the development calls) is 0.015 above the read-out on average (0.991, 0.841, 0.950, 0.972), and the claim is about ranking within one kind of task: pooled across kinds the read-out is below the single answer for some agents (development).
\item \textbf{The second benchmark for Claude (G18): design and limits.} $\tau^2$-bench airline \citep{barres2025tau2}, Sierra's published domain, policy and tasks; Claude Haiku~5.5 as the agent and as the simulated customer, reasoning off. Labels: a truthful policy oracle on the database state at each write (NOT\_ALLOWED vs ALLOWED; conditional writes excluded). Data: 360 writes (every violation and allowed writes drawn uniformly) from five runs: $\tau^2$'s own customer on 30 tasks, a customer who insists after a refusal on 20 tasks, and 30 more trials of the 8 tasks where an earlier Claude run had violated (chosen from labels of earlier episodes, never from answers). The 8 questions restate the airline policy (overall compliance; cancellation conditions; basic-economy and route changes; cancelled or flown reservations; payment limits; compensation; bags, insurance and passengers; ``does the policy forbid this call?''). Smoke runs on 30 other writes changed three things before the lock: a fixed note in the system prompt says that a message marked [Operator check] comes from the operator and that the customer does not see it (in the customer's turn, Claude declined G15's frame, or took a named sender for an injection); a first line that begins with the answer is parsed first; and two wordings of the last question were replaced. 93\% of answers parsed. Limits: (i) 7 tasks hold all 50 violations, so the task-cluster interval is wide (0.54 to 0.82). (ii) Within one task and tool the read-out scores 0.61 [0.46, 0.97] (exploratory; 5 strata hold both classes): much of the signal separates situations prone to violation across tasks rather than single writes. (iii) Without the targeted run it scores 0.73 (18 violations; exploratory). (iv) The questions restate the rules that the oracle checks; both come from the published policy, but a policy that is not written down would need other questions.
\item \textbf{The law over-predicts Claude (G12) by sampling error in $\tau$.} No violation ran after a block in any of Claude's gate episodes. Its live flag rate on mailing-list sends was 0.12 [0.09, 0.17], against 0.21 [0.11, 0.36] in the offline audit (8 of 38 units); with the live rates the law gives 0.31 (observed 0.30). Audit enough offline units for each kind of task.
\item \textbf{Self-policing has four limits.} (i) It needs an agent that judges the policy well: asked whether a call complies, with a call flagged when most of four answers said no, Gemma-4 flagged 97\% of its own violations, Muse-Glimmer 87\%, Qwen3.8 47\%, gpt-oss 37\% and Claude 5\%; Claude needed a rule-by-rule check (development data). (ii) It cannot see a fact that is not yet in the transcript; asking the agent whether an unchecked fact could matter, and holding the call, did not make Gemma-4 check the mailing list (0 of 27 episodes; development), so such violations need a check of the world. (iii) In G10 the rule-by-rule audit had 700 output tokens; 9.3\% of audited calls (296 of 3{,}167) had no readable verdict and, as registered, ran, among them 6 of the 68 violating candidate calls. The released tool allows 2{,}048 tokens and holds such calls. (iv) The reviewer was CAPE's checker, a perfect reviewer; G9's resampling arm needed 0.404 reviews per episode against a preregistered limit of 0.40, so that endpoint failed. One closed agent and one benchmark were tested.
\item \textbf{On Claude, the catch rate measures two behaviours.} Claude Haiku~5.5's 68 violations are 42 test sends to a
  mailing list (every such send violates; the list's thousands of external members appear only after sending, though a
  command would have shown them) and 26 production restarts without \texttt{request\_permission}. A prior that knows only each
  template's and tool's violation rate, fit out of fold by scenario, catches 91.6\% [82.1, 95.9] at the 5\% budget (the reader: 94.1\%;
  AUROC 0.992 vs 0.989, difference $-0.003$ [$-0.009$, $+0.003$]). Two thirds of the violations sit in a template where every call violates, and the
  AUROC within templates rests on one template (26 violations). The mailing-list sends are decided by a fact that is not in the transcript at the time of
  the action, so a monitor of the transcript can flag them only through a prior over that fact, such as the template's rate. GPT-6-luna's violations span six mixed templates, where the
  reader beats the prior (98.5\% vs 86.4\%; AUROC $+0.032$ [0.007, 0.064]); on Gemma-4 it beats it by $+0.077$ [0.064, 0.091] on the same scenarios and $+0.044$ [0.028, 0.060] on unseen task families, where its AUROC within templates is 0.917 [0.903, 0.930]. Within templates, TF-IDF with the same labels ties the reader except on unseen task families (0.923 vs the reader's 0.965 pooled); the reader's advantage over word features is robustness to shift (exploratory; scenario-cluster bootstrap).
\item \textbf{Reading the agent's reasoning (G8, preregistered, mixed).} On the held-out scenarios of three reasoning agents, trained on the agent's own labels, the reader with the reasoning changes the AUROC by \GEightInagent{} on average; non-superiority at $+0.01$ fails, because Qwen3.8 gains \GEightQwen{}. Trained on Gemma-4 only, which is how a new agent is monitored, the reasoning lowers the AUROC by \GEightTransfer{} (per agent \GEightTransferEach{}). For violations later in the episode, which the action does not decide, both monitors are weak (\GEightFutureLevel{}) and the reasoning's effect is \GEightFuture{}: too few positives to say.
\item \textbf{\Cref{cor:action} needs a label that the action and the visible facts decide.} It does not cover labels about
  later behaviour or beliefs (for later behaviour, the rollout forecast dominates every readout; \cref{prop:behaviour}).
\item \textbf{Scores do not transfer between agents}: on Claude, a cut at probability 0.5 catches 3\%. The cut must come
  from the monitored agent's own traffic (\cref{prop:frozen}).
\item \textbf{The live tests compare the gate with no gate}; no baseline gate ran in the loop. Offline, on the same agents'
  991- and 1{,}000-episode runs, holding 5\% of actions would have stopped 94\%/98\% (Claude/GPT) of the episodes with a violation
  with the reader, 64\%/49\% with TF-IDF, 0\%/38\% with the 70B judge and 42\%/14\% with tool identity (exploratory).
\item \textbf{The monitor is not robust to an adaptive attacker.} An attacker who queries the score and rewrites free text evades it; both preregistered defenses failed (below). Keep the score hidden from the agent.
\item \textbf{Blocking costs goals.} Goals reached fell from 47.4\% to 45.6\% (GPT) and from 40.7\% to 34.9\% (Claude); holding
  for an oracle reviewer gave 38.7\%, and the preregistered test that review costs at most 3 points was not shown
  ($-0.020$ [$-0.051$, $+0.007$]). A human reviewer is slower and makes errors.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Design order}
Several tests were designed after earlier results: the second $\tau^2$-bench tests after the first, the reasoning judge after
the unseen-task test, the canonical form after the adaptive attack, and adversarial training after the canonical form. Each was preregistered and locked before its own data
existed, and the later locks were published first, but their hypotheses were not independent of what we had seen.
